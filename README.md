# labor-econ-remote-work-analysis-hours

**Did Remote Work Narrow the Motherhood Penalty? Evidence on the Intensive Margin from Israeli Labor Force Survey Microdata, 2017–2023.**

Inbal Moryles and Nitzan Zacharia — seminar paper, Tel Aviv University (Seminar in Topics in Labor Economics and the Economics of Education), to be submitted 15 October 2026. Source: `paper/paper.tex`; compiled copy: `paper/paper.pdf`. The first author's name is spelled Moryles; an early planning document spelled it differently, and that spelling is wrong.

Empirical analysis using Israeli Central Bureau of Statistics (CBS) Labor Force Survey microdata to test whether the post-COVID shift to remote work altered the motherhood penalty in **usual weekly work hours** among employed women. A mother/childless × pre/post-2021 difference-in-differences (DiD) estimates the average change; a triple-differences (DDD) model that interacts it with an Israeli-calibrated occupational WFH-exposure index tests whether the change is concentrated where the work can be done from home. Headline: no average change (DiD 0.23 hours, SE 0.18), but a 3.22-hour-per-unit-of-exposure gradient (SE 1.02; analytic *p* = 0.003, permutation *p* = 0.032, wild-cluster-bootstrap *p* = 0.056), or 1.71 hours between the mean exposures of the most and least teleworkable quartiles of occupations. The employment margin is estimated too imprecisely to support a comparable conclusion.

## Background

**Motivation.** Israel combines high labor productivity with birth rates that are exceptionally high compared to the rest of the OECD, so changes in the motherhood penalty carry outsized economic weight. The findings are intended to inform local policy debates on gender equality, maternity leave, and flexible work arrangements.

**The motherhood penalty.** A well-documented negative effect of childbirth on women's wages and employment, opening a persistent gap between mothers and both men and childless women (long-term wage penalties of roughly 20–40% in the literature). It's driven by mothers shifting to part-time/"mommy track" roles, an unequal household/childcare burden, and "commitment bias" discrimination by employers who perceive mothers as less committed.
- Correll et al. (2007): mothers face wage/hiring discrimination from a "less committed" stereotype; fathers see the opposite — a wage premium.
- Kleven et al. (2019, Denmark): first childbirth causes a ~20% long-term income decline for women, via reduced hours, labor-market exit, or a shift to lower-paying family-friendly jobs.
- Kleven et al. (2019, cross-country): the penalty is universal but its severity tracks cultural/gender norms, steepest where mothers are expected to stay home.
- Harrington & Kahn (2026, *National Tax Journal*): rising remote work is shrinking the penalty — higher employment for mothers in demanding professions, less post-birth dropout, easier work/parenting integration.

**Why remote work might change it.** The pandemic pushed work-from-home from ~5% to ~25–30% of workdays. This could narrow the penalty (less commute friction, more schedule flexibility, reduced stigma around physical availability) or widen it (lower visibility, slower advancement, new remote-specific stigma) — which is why this is an empirical question rather than an assumed direction.

## Research design

The core analysis is a mother/childless × pre/post-2021 difference-in-differences design, using the COVID-era shift toward remote work as the "post" treatment period:

$$Y_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{Mother}_i + \beta_2 \cdot \text{Post}_t + \beta_3 \cdot (\text{Mother}_i \times \text{Post}_t) + X'_{it}\gamma + \varepsilon_{it}$$

- **Y**: weekly work hours (intensive margin, **primary**) and employment (extensive margin, secondary).
- **Mother**: 1 for women with a child aged 17 or younger in the household (treatment group); 0 for childless women (control group).
- **Post**: 1 for 2021–2023 (post-shift), 0 for 2017–2019 (baseline); 2020 is excluded as a transitional year.
- **β₃**: the DiD estimator — the differential post-shift change in outcomes for mothers vs. childless women. For the primary (hours) specification, a significant positive β₃ indicates a narrowing of the penalty on the intensive margin; the same estimator on the secondary (employment) specification indicates a narrowing extensive-margin penalty.
- **X'**: controls (education, age group, marital status, religion, district — see `DEFAULT_CONTROLS` below); errors are clustered by individual (`IDPUF`).

Both the primary (hours) DiD/DDD and the secondary (employment) regression are estimated on the full pooled sample and separately for Jewish and Arab women (`Leom == 1` / `Leom == 2`), to check whether the effect differs by population group; the hours split is the paper's Table 5, with two-sample *z*-tests across the subsamples.

The project implements a fuller empirical strategy, built in fifteen checkpoints between early September and 2026-09-23:
- the **primary intensive-margin** regression on usual weekly work hours (conditional on employment), plus
  a Lee (2009) trimming-bounds correction (`intensive_margin_lee_bounds.R`) for the selection risk
  that conditioning on employment introduces. The bounds are DiD-adapted: the counterfactual selection
  rate for post-period mothers is their pre-period rate plus the childless women's pre/post change, and
  the excess is trimmed from the top and bottom of the hours distribution. Each bound carries its own
  cluster-robust SE and the pair carries an Imbens–Manski (2004) confidence interval. The construction
  bounds excess selection in one direction only (differential pull into employment); under-selection in
  the post-period mothers' cell is not addressed,
- a **primary triple-differences (DDD) mechanism regression on hours** (`WorkHoursCont ~ Mother×Post×WFH_Exposure`, pure occupation-level exposure), plus a generalized Lee-bounds correction and Imbens-Manski CI, testing whether the narrowing penalty is actually driven by an occupation's remote-work exposure. Hours became the primary outcome on 2026-09-12 for two reasons: the employment DDD's minimum detectable effect was about a quarter of baseline employment, roughly four times its point estimate, and the forty-occupation exposure regressor is only usable for an outcome that is already conditional on employment (occupation is undefined for the non-employed; for hours that conditioning is intrinsic to the question, which is also why the Lee correction is needed). Unconditional (default pipeline run, no feature flag) as of the pivot,
- the same **DDD mechanism regression on employment** (`Employed ~ Mother×Post×WFH_Exposure`, cell-based exposure) as the secondary specification, cross-referenced against literature anchors (Bloom; Cohen & Manor 2024). The three occupation-level robustness variants of this secondary DDD (calibrated/external/realized exposure, `ddd_regression.R`) were deprecated and removed on 2026-09-12,
- the same DiD/DDD on the **male subsample** (fathers vs. childless men, "Mother" read as "Father"). The code and the exported files still call this a *placebo* (`run_hours_gender_placebo()`, `run_gender_placebo()`, `*_gender_placebo_*.csv`), but the paper (Empirical Strategy §5.5, Results §6.5, Table 5) reads it as a **comparison population, not a placebo**: fathers in teleworkable occupations are exposed to remote work exactly as mothers are, so a null was never the prediction. What the paper reports is that the fathers' hours-DDD gradient has the *opposite* sign (−1.762, SE 1.015, analytic *p* = 0.09, wild-bootstrap *p* = 0.15) and is distinguishable from the mothers' (*z* = 3.461) — evidence against a common macro shock in teleworkable occupations, and consistent with (not proof of) within-household reallocation. Both runs are unconditional in `main.R` and export their DiD and DDD tables: the hours (primary) one via `run_hours_gender_placebo()`, and the employment (secondary) one via `run_gender_placebo()`, wired in 2026-09-19 so both margins carry the same comparison,
- and **heterogeneity by the age of the youngest child** (`run_hours_ddd_by_child_age()`, paper Table 4): the hours DiD/DDD re-estimated for mothers whose youngest child is 0–4, 5–9, 10–14 or 15–17, each against all childless women, on the premise that younger children demand more intensive care — the mechanism's most direct test (the DDD gradient declines monotonically with child age). The older employment-trajectory-by-child-age descriptives (`employment_by_child_age()`) still run and reach `outputs/`, but were cut from the paper on 2026-09-21. They are raw levels with no control group, and must not be read against the employment DiD.

Two methodological gaps between the original research plan and the actual CBS extract were resolved as recorded decisions rather than left ambiguous, and both are disclosed in the paper:
- the WFH-exposure index anchors to **2021**, not the originally-planned 2020, because no 2020 raw extract exists for this project. The 2021 index therefore measures post-adaptation adoption rather than teleworkability at the moment of the shock,
- the age control is the categorical **`GilNK`** age-group code (five bins across ages 25–59), not continuous age/age², because no continuous age or birth-year variable exists in the raw extract. Five bins are a coarser functional form than a quadratic, which matters when comparing with literature that uses continuous age.

The calibrated and realized occupation-level exposure measures are built on employed women **and men** aged 25–59 (`exposure_population_df` in `main.R`), not on the women-only analysis sample, so that an occupation's score does not depend on the outcome sample it is later applied to.

## Data

- **Source**: Israeli CBS Labor Force Survey microdata. Raw files are yearly CSVs with Hebrew-transliterated variable names (e.g. `Muasak` = employed, `AvodaMeHaBayit` = works from home, `Leom` = population group).
- **Not included in this repo**: raw CSVs are gitignored and must be supplied locally. Edit `folder_path` at the top of `main.R` to point at your local data folder.
- **Sample**: women aged 25–59 by default, survey years 2017–2019 and 2021–2023 (2020 excluded — no raw extract exists for that year). `load_and_clean_data(folder_path, sex_filter = "men")` builds the analogous male subsample used for exposure construction and the fathers' comparison (the "gender placebo" in code).
- **Caching**: the first run cleans the raw CSVs and saves the result as `cleaned_df.rds` in the data folder, alongside a small `cleaned_df.rds.meta.rds` sidecar recording a hash of `data_processing.R`. Subsequent runs reuse the cache automatically, but only while that hash still matches — if `data_processing.R` has changed since the cache was built, `main.R` detects the mismatch and rebuilds automatically, so no manual delete step is needed. The male subsample that `main.R` §8 loads for exposure construction and the fathers' comparison is cached the same way, as `cleaned_df_men.rds` with its own `.meta.rds` sidecar keyed to the same hash.
- **Schema-drift guard**: before a fresh (non-cached) load, `check_schema_drift()` verifies that a handful of name-bounded column ranges — used by positional `select(-(a:b))` drops in `data_processing.R` — occupy the same columns across every year's CSV, so a future CBS format change fails loudly instead of silently dropping the wrong data.
- **Validation guard**: every load (cached or fresh) is checked by `validate_cleaned_df()`, which hard-fails (`stop()`) on impossible states (wrong sex code, out-of-range age group, a stray 2020 row, NAs in `Employed`/`Mother`/`Post`, zero rows) and warns on soft thresholds (a regression control with >5% NA, etc.).

### Variable contracts and validation rules

The cleaned frame has 87 columns. Only the derived columns below and the columns the regressions consume have a defined contract; the ~65 raw passthrough columns keep whatever type `read_csv()` guessed and mean what the CBS codebook (`H20231031Codebook.xlsx`, held with the raw data) says they mean.

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| WFH items | CBS yes/no items code 1 = yes, 2 = no, 9 = unknown; code 9 becomes `NA`, never 0. `WFH` is usual work location (`AvodaMeHaBayit`); `WFH_RefWeek` is reference-week behaviour (`AvadMeHaBayit`), asked only of those who worked that week, so the employed-but-absent are `NA`. `WFH_Share` is home hours over actual hours, capped at 1, `NA` when either hour item carries a CBS code in the 90s ("irregular"/"unknown"). All are `NA` before 2021 by design. |
| `WorkHoursCont` | Bin medians for usual-hours codes 1–10 (range 4–78.5; the open 60+ bin at 78.5). The irregular-hours codes 11 and 12 are imputed from the observed median of the matching range, computed **separately within each `Post` period** so the imputation cannot blend the pre- and post-period hours distributions. Codes 0 and 99 are `NA`. Defined only for `Employed == 1 & AvadBeshavua == 1`, so its NA rate is about 23% (not employed) plus 8% (employed but absent) in every year, by design. |
| Collapsed categoricals | `TeudaGvoha`: 11 raw codes to 6 levels, `NA` only for code 99. `BirthContinent`: 16 raw codes to 6 levels, `NA` only for code 16 (CBS's ambiguous "other/unknown"). `ISCO_masked` records a disclosure mask (`XX`, `7X`, …) explicitly instead of a silent `NA`; `ISCO1` recovers the one-digit major group where partial masking preserves it. |
| Hard-fail checks | Every row has the requested sex code; `GilNK` in 3–7 (ages 25–59); survey year in {2017, 2018, 2019, 2021, 2022, 2023}; zero `NA` in `Employed`, `Mother`, `Post`; a non-empty frame. Any failure means the derivation logic broke, not that the data is missing. |
| Soft-fail checks | A regression control with more than 5% `NA` (the highest today is `TeudaGvoha` at 2.1%); a descriptive-only variable with more than 70% `NA` (the WFH block sits near 64–69% because it is undefined before 2021); any survey year with more than 2% of the employed at raw usual-hours code 0 or 99, excluding the documented 2017 exception. That last rule is the signature of the 2017 defect described under "Research design", keyed on the raw code because the post-harmonization `NA` rate is high by design. |
| Schema-drift check | `data_processing.R` drops five column ranges positionally. `check_schema_drift()` reads each year's header and asserts that the boundary columns of every range (`Yeladim0_1Prat`/`Yeladim15_17Prat`, `MisparHachlafa`/`YachasKirvaNK`, `MisparNefashotGilAvodaV2007`/`MisparPrat`, `ChipusAvodaSherutTaasuka`/`ChipusAvodaOfenAcher`, `RamatDat`/`BituachLeumi`) sit in the same relative positions in every file. Two further ranges were converted to name-based drops because the 2017 file lacks their boundary columns entirely. |

## Setup & running

Dependencies: R with the `tidyverse` and `fixest` packages (no lockfile/renv), plus `fwildclusterboot` (which pulls in `dqrng`) for the wild-cluster-bootstrap p-values on the hours DDD (`robustness/hours_ddd_inference.R`, added 2026-09-22). `fwildclusterboot` was archived from CRAN in May 2024, so it is installed from its GitHub repository. The bootstrap is the only thing that needs it: it is loaded inside the function with `requireNamespace()`, and the test suite skips the bootstrap tests when it is absent. Project rule: do not add any other dependency without flagging it first. `car`, for instance, was considered for the VIF diagnostic and replaced by base R (`lm()`, `kappa()`).

```r
install.packages(c("tidyverse", "fixest", "remotes"))
remotes::install_github("s3alfisc/fwildclusterboot")
```

Then edit `folder_path` in `main.R` to your local data folder and run:

```r
source("main.R")
```

or from a shell: `Rscript main.R`.

This runs the full default pipeline, in `main.R`'s section order:

1. **Load, cache and validate** (§3): raw CSVs → `cleaned_df` (women aged 25–59), behind the schema-drift and validation guards described under "Data"; the men's frame is loaded and cached in §8.
2. **Descriptives** (§4): comparative statistics and the paper's Table 1 (`build_descriptive_table()`).
3. **DiD regressions** (§5): the primary hours DiD plus its Lee (2009) selection bounds and Imbens–Manski CI; the secondary employment DiD, pooled and for Jewish and Arab women.
4. **Employment by child age** (§6), then the **event studies and joint pre-trend Wald tests** for both outcomes (§7).
5. **Exposure measures** (§8): external (Dingel & Neiman), calibrated (primary) and realized (2021 anchor) at the occupation level, plus the pre-period demographic-cell index; the ISCO-masking sensitivity check.
6. **Primary hours DDD and everything built on it** (§8a): the headline model and its MDE; the DDD event study and its Wald test; the generalized Lee bounds; the external, realized, unswapped-occupations and excluding-2023 variants; two-way clustering; the saturated and quartile-binned specifications; the year-FE DiD; the outcome-coding checks (no imputation, ≥35 hours, ≥40 hours, and since the 2026-09-28 referee review the open 60+ bin recoded to 60 and a top-bin indicator) and the absentees-at-usual-hours refit on 2018–2023 (`WorkHoursUsualAll`); the calibration tests (men-only calibration, the external index with teaching alone reclassified, the gap-threshold sweep from 0.30 to 0.90, fixed-sample swap control); the occupational-sorting DiD and the cell-exposure bound; the forty leave-one-occupation-out refits; the pre-period balance table by exposure quartile; the occupation-level first stage; the Jewish/Arab hours splits; the fathers' comparison on both margins; the subgroup forest plots; and the child-age heterogeneity split.
7. **Secondary employment DDD** (§8b: cell-based exposure, two specifications, runtime collinearity diagnostic); the **age-balance robustness chain** (§8c, `RUN_AGE_BALANCE_ROBUSTNESS`), followed unconditionally by the two **marital-balance specs** (married women only; marital status × Post × WFH_Exposure added); the **null-vs-power audit** (§8d, `RUN_NULL_VS_POWER_AUDIT`: first-stage relevance and the employment MDEs); and **small-cluster inference** on the hours DDD (§8f): the wild cluster bootstrap (9,999 draws, unconditional) on the headline, the DDD pre-trend coefficients and every one-way occupation-clustered refit that feeds Tables 3–5 (its *p* (boot) column; a refit the bootstrap cannot handle is reported and left blank rather than stopping the run), and the permutation test (`RUN_PERMUTATION_TEST`, `N_PERMUTATIONS = 999` refits, several minutes — set it to 99 for a dev run; the exported table records `n_perm` and the seed, so a reduced run cannot be mistaken for the paper's number).
8. **Paper figure set** (§8e) and **paper tables assembled** (§8g), then **export** (§9): every result to `outputs/` as CSV/PNG, the paper's figures a second time as vector PDFs to `outputs/figures/`, and the paper's tables to `paper/tables/*.tex` (see "Outputs" and "Paper" below).

All three flags default to `TRUE`, so a default run reproduces every number the paper cites. Every piece of the empirical strategy runs from this one command; nothing is invoked manually. (The employment-outcome fathers' comparison included: `main.R` runs `run_gender_placebo()` unconditionally, reusing the cached men's frame, and exports `gender_placebo_{did,ddd}_table.csv`.) Two manual-only scripts were removed on 2026-09-28: `basic_reg_compared_data.R` (the employment DiD on `Muasak`-observed rows, never called and never cited) and `israeli_market_mismatch.R` with its `run_mismatch.R` entry point (a women-only restatement of `calibrate_isco_exposure()`'s output, superseded by `outputs/wfh_exposure_calibrated.csv`); both are in git history.

### Reproducing a run

- **Inputs and time.** The raw CBS CSVs live outside the repo (`folder_path` in `main.R`). A full default run takes about 25–27 minutes and peaks near 7 GB of memory; most of the time is the 999 permutation refits. `N_PERMUTATIONS` can be set to 99 for a development run; the exported permutation table records `n_perm` and the seed, so a reduced run cannot be mistaken for the paper's number.
- **Caches.** The cleaned women's and men's frames are cached beside the raw data (`cleaned_df.rds`, `cleaned_df_men.rds`), each with a `.meta.rds` sidecar holding the MD5 of `scripts/data_processing.R`. A cache is reused only while that hash matches, so editing the cleaning script rebuilds it automatically.
- **Seeds.** `INFERENCE_SEED` (20260922, `main.R` section 8f) seeds both `dqrng::dqset.seed()`, which the wild bootstrap draws from, and `set.seed()`, which the permutation test uses. `set.seed()` alone does not reproduce `boottest()`.
- **What moves on an unchanged tree.** A re-run with no code change rewrites all eight PDFs (`paper/paper.pdf` aside): both base `pdf()` devices and the six `cairo_pdf` figures are nondeterministic. Every CSV and PNG is byte-stable. Establish that no-op baseline before reading a re-run's `git diff`, or the PDF churn is indistinguishable from signal.
- **Invariants after a hours-chain change.** The four `outputs/wfh_exposure_*.csv` files, both Lee-bounds selection-rate files and the entire employment margin must stay byte-identical; any of them moving means the change leaked outside `WorkHoursCont`. The last two pipeline changes kept to this: only the two descriptive-table CSVs moved on 2026-09-22 (new SE columns) and only the three MDE files on 2026-09-23 (new `df` and `multiplier` columns).
- **Generated tables.** `tex_coef_cell()` prints a fixed number of decimals (`formatC(x, digits, format = "f")`, three for the hours tables and four for the employment table) rather than mirroring `etable()`; `tests/testthat/test-tex_coef_cell.R` pins that. Every DDD runner returns `n_clusters`, so the cluster-count column of the tables travels with the model rather than being typed in.

### Tests

```r
Rscript run_tests.R
```

Runs the `testthat` suite in `tests/testthat/` (data processing, validation, schema-drift, every regression function, export, and a full-pipeline smoke test) against fixture CSVs checked into the repo, exiting non-zero on any failure. As of 2026-10-03 the suite is 67 files and 1,707 passing expectations with one documented skip. Any change touching a function used elsewhere (`data_processing.R`, the controls list) should be green on this suite before being considered done. One test is skipped by default: the block of `test-wfh_crosswalk_integrity.R` that rebuilds `data/israeli_cbs_wfh_2digit.csv` from its two public source files. To run it, set `WFH_DN_OCCUPATIONS_CSV` and `WFH_BLS_ISCO_SOC_XLS` to local copies of those files (it checks their SHA-256 first, and needs `readxl`, which is used nowhere else).

## Project structure

`main.R` and `run_tests.R` are the top-level entry-point scripts and stay at the repo root; every single-function file lives under `scripts/` and is `source()`d with a root-relative, path-qualified call (`source(file.path("scripts", "foo.R"))`), since cwd is assumed to be the repo root wherever sourcing happens. Five files in `scripts/` hold more than one function, each for a stated reason (`validation.R`, `wfh_exposure_cells.R`, `gender_placebo.R`, `hours_gender_placebo.R`, `ddd_collinearity_diagnostics.R`); the one-function rule applies to new files.

`robustness/` holds the multi-function robustness-chain scripts, covering both the primary (hours) and secondary (employment) DDDs — `balance_test.R`, `age_balance_robustness.R`, `pretrend_wald_test.R`, and, since 2026-09-22, `hours_ddd_inference.R` — each defining several related functions, so they don't fit `scripts/`'s convention. They are sourced the same path-qualified way (`source(file.path("robustness", "foo.R"))`) and are never moved into `scripts/`. `pretrend_wald_test.R` and the wild bootstrap in `hours_ddd_inference.R` run unconditionally (the F-statistics are the paper's parallel-trends evidence; the bootstrap is one of its three inference procedures); `balance_test.R`/`age_balance_robustness.R` run under `RUN_AGE_BALANCE_ROBUSTNESS` and the permutation test under `RUN_PERMUTATION_TEST`, both defaulting to `TRUE` so a default run reproduces everything the paper cites. A further robustness script, `phase2_robustness.R`, was never wired in and was removed on 2026-09-12.

`data/` holds small, versioned external inputs the pipeline needs: the Dingel & Neiman teleworkability score at the two-digit ISCO-08 level (`israeli_cbs_wfh_2digit.csv`) and the ISCO-08 two-digit titles used by the appendix table (`isco08_2digit_labels.csv`). The score file is Dingel & Neiman's (2020) published binary indicator (`occupations_workathome.csv` in their replication repository, github.com/jdingel/DingelNeiman-workathome), mapped to ISCO-08 through the Bureau of Labor Statistics' 2012 crosswalk between ISCO-08 and the 2010 SOC (bls.gov/soc/soccrosswalks.htm; the author's copy was supplied by the Israeli CBS) and averaged without weights over the matched pairs within each two-digit group. Rebuilding it from those two public files reproduces it exactly on all 43 rows; the derivation, the two input files' SHA-256 pins and the executable proof are in `tests/testthat/test-wfh_crosswalk_integrity.R`. The two source files are not committed (D&N's repository is GPLv3; the BLS file is an `.xls`).

## Outputs

`Rscript main.R` writes one file per result table/plot to `outputs/` (CSV for tables, PNG for plots) via `export_all_results()`. `outputs/` is **tracked in git**, not gitignored.

The handful of figures the paper actually includes are then written a **second** time, as vector PDFs under `outputs/figures/`, by `export_paper_figures()` (`scripts/export_paper_figures.R`). This is deliberate, not redundant: the PNG is the browsing copy at a fixed 8×5in/150dpi, while `paper/paper.tex` compiles against the PDF at the ~5in width it is printed at. Two consequences worth knowing before editing either side — the keys of `main.R`'s `paper_figures` list **are** the filenames `paper.tex` hard-codes, so renaming one breaks the LaTeX build; and the paper figures are exported with their in-plot title/subtitle/caption stripped, because in a LaTeX float that text is the job of `\caption` and the `Notes` block.

Nothing row-level is exported. The per-person tables behind `check_idpuf_panel_structure()`, the men's frame and the age-balance chain's row-level `pre_df` frames stay in memory; `outputs/` holds aggregates only.

## Known limitations

- **Survey weights are intentionally not applied in any regression.** CBS weight columns (`MishkalSofi`, `MishkalShnati`, etc.) exist in the raw data and are deliberately excluded from every regression in this repo (`basic_reg()`, the intensive-margin/DDD models, the pretrend model, etc.) — this is a scope decision, not an oversight, and should not be "fixed" without a separate discussion. Reported rates and regression coefficients are unweighted estimates on the analysis sample, not population-representative statistics. (Four uses of a `weights =` argument do exist, and none of them is a survey weight. `build_exposure_cells()` in `wfh_exposure_cells.R` weights by `MishkalSofi` when aggregating occupation exposure up to demographic cells — internal to building the exposure regressor, not a representativeness correction. `run_ddd_reweighted()` and `run_hours_ddd_reweighted()` in `robustness/age_balance_robustness.R` pass `weights = ~rake_weight` to `feols()`, which is the entire point of those two age-rebalancing specs; the reweighted row of the paper's robustness table comes from them. `run_hours_ddd_regression()` uses inverse-variance weights in the second-stage `lm()` over per-occupation estimates. And `build_wfh_occupation_first_stage()` weights its forty-occupation first-stage slope by occupation cell size. The claim above is specifically about CBS *survey* weights in an outcome regression, and that still holds everywhere.)
- The WFH-exposure index and age controls both deviate from the original research plan's literal specification, as documented decisions (see "Research design"), because the raw CBS extract lacks a 2020 file and any continuous age/birth-year variable.
- **The Lee bounds are one-directional.** Both Lee-bounds corrections trim for excess selection into the observed-hours sample (WFH pulling marginal mothers into work after 2021); under-selection in the post-period mothers' cell is not bounded.
- **Person identifiers can repeat across years.** `IDPUF` is not guaranteed unique across survey years, so the same person can contribute rows on both sides of the `Post` divide. `check_idpuf_panel_structure()` reports how much this happens; nothing corrects for it, and the DiD standard errors are clustered on `IDPUF`.
- **The ISCO-masking check is a proxy.** CBS masks thin occupation cells (`XX`, `7X`, …; 2.4% of the analysis sample), and the exposure index drops those rows. `check_isco_masking_sensitivity()` compares masked and unmasked rows within the one-digit major group that partial masking preserves; a fully masked row carries no occupation signal at any resolution.

The behavioural specification is the test suite (`tests/testthat/`, run via `Rscript run_tests.R`); the paper's own change log is the dated comment block at the top of `paper/paper.tex`; `paper/notes/conclusion-audit-2026-09-28.md` is the one analysis note kept in the repository. The design documents, decision memos, roadmap, original research plan and referee review that guided the build are kept outside version control from 2026-10-03; the repository's history before that date holds them, and the conclusions a reader needs are stated above. The original plan's research question, literature review and empirical design are folded into "Background" and "Research design"; where the implementation departs from the plan's literal specification (the 2021 anchor year, the categorical age control, the mobility variable kept for description but excluded from every regression), the departure is recorded in this file and disclosed in the paper.

Three external assessments shaped the current pipeline and none is in the repository: two seminar grade reports (2026-09-22 and 2026-09-23, answered by the specification, inference, heterogeneity and generated-table work of those dates) and a referee review of 2026-09-28 with twenty-two items on what the paper claims about its own design, each with recomputed evidence and a suggested change. All twenty-two were closed by 2026-09-29: items 1–3, 6, 10 and 11 added rows, an appendix table or an export through the pipeline (marital balance, bootstrap *p*-values on the supporting rows, the teaching-only swap and threshold sweep, the top-bin and absentees rows, the masking regression); the rest were text corrections. Two of the review's proposals were deliberately scaled down: ISCO 21 rather than ISCO 26 is named as the gradient's main carrier, because the repo's own leave-one-out refits contradict the review's unsaved decomposition, and trimming bounds on the absence margin are deferred and said to be deferred in Limitations. The review also tested and set aside several alternatives (Haredi composition, an own-children rather than household-children definition of `Mother`, a trend-adjusted DDD, two-way clustering, the child-age gradient as an age artifact); those computations were never saved and none of their numbers appears in the paper. Every number in the paper enters through `main.R` and `build_paper_tables()`, never from an external computation.
