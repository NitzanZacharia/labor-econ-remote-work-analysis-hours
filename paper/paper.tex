\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\citet{harrington2025} ask whether the post-pandemic rise of work from home (WFH) reduced the
motherhood penalty in the United States. This paper takes that question to Israel and to a
different margin of labor supply. Using Central Bureau of Statistics (CBS) Labor Force Survey
microdata on women aged 25--59 in 2017--2019 and 2021--2023, we estimate difference-in-differences
(DiD) and triple-differences (DDD) models of employment and of usual weekly work hours for mothers
relative to childless women, using the COVID-19-driven shift to remote work as the ``post'' period
and a WFH-exposure index as the third difference (occupation-level for the hours model,
demographic-cell-based for the employment model). On the extensive margin, the
mother--non-mother employment gap did not change ($\Mother \times \Post = -0.0053$, SE $0.0052$),
and the employment DDD is underpowered rather than informative (its minimum detectable effect is
roughly eight to ten times its own point estimate). On the intensive margin, employed mothers'
weekly hours did not change on average either ($\Mother \times \Post = 0.23$, SE $0.18$). The
effect is instead \emph{confined} to teleworkable work: the triple interaction with WFH exposure is
$3.22$ hours per unit of exposure (SE $1.02$, $p<0.01$), while the hours change for mothers at zero
exposure is if anything negative. That estimate is robust to a generalized trimming-bounds
correction for selection into the estimation sample, with a 95\% confidence interval for the
identified set of $[1.02, 5.32]$, and fathers show no corresponding exposure gradient. We read this
as WFH access relaxing a binding time constraint for mothers who remain employed in teleworkable
jobs --- and, because there is no average effect to speak of, as a result about \emph{which} mothers
benefit rather than about mothers as a group.
\end{abstract}

%======================================================================
\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================

\citet{harrington2025} pose a simple question for the United States: has the rise of work from
home reduced the motherhood penalty in the labor market? Their answer is yes on the extensive
margin and on income --- a 10\% rise in WFH raises mothers' employment by roughly $0.78$ percentage
points relative to other women, concentrated in careers with high returns to hours and inflexible
time demands, and employed mothers' incomes rise by about $1.3\%$ relative to other employed women.
This paper asks the same question of a different country and a different margin. We use Israeli
CBS Labor Force Survey microdata to test whether the post-COVID shift to remote work altered the
motherhood penalty in Israel, and our headline result concerns \emph{usual weekly work hours}
among employed women rather than employment or income. It is therefore an extension of
\citeauthor{harrington2025}'s design to another labor market and to the intensive margin, not a
direct replication of their US finding: we also estimate the employment margin, but --- for
reasons of statistical power that we document rather than paper over --- that specification is
secondary.

The question carries both scientific and policy significance. Scientifically, the sudden, large,
and plausibly exogenous shift in the location of work constitutes a rare natural experiment in
workplace flexibility. This is the very margin that \citet{goldin2014} identifies as the last
major driver of the remaining gender pay gap in developed labor markets. Temporal and locational
flexibility is a good mothers may pay for, in the form of lower wages or slower promotion. A shock
that lowers the cost of supplying flexibility should, in principle, be detectable in mothers'
labor-supply choices --- and, for mothers already employed, most directly in how many hours they
supply. Unlike the acute, transitory disruption of 2020, WFH has proven to be a persistent feature
of the post-pandemic labor market rather than a temporary accommodation \citep{barrero2021}. This
renders the question policy-relevant rather than merely historical.

The policy stakes are particularly high in Israel, which combines a highly productive,
internationally integrated labor market with the OECD's highest total fertility rate. A labor
market that induces mothers of young children to reduce employment or hours imposes a substantial
aggregate cost. This cost is especially large in a country where a large and growing share of the
working-age population is, at any given time, a parent of young children. Conversely, flexible
work arrangements might measurably narrow the gap between mothers and other workers. If so, the
finding bears directly on the design of remote-work policy, parental-leave rules, and public
discourse on gender equality in the labor market. At the same time, Israeli
institutional practice diverges sharply from US task-based predictions of which jobs can be done
remotely --- schools stayed in person by policy, for example --- which matters for how ``WFH
exposure'' should be measured and is one of the methodological contributions below.

Our research design exploits the pandemic as the moment at which remote work became a mainstream
labor-market practice. Before 2020, working from home accounted for roughly 5\% of workdays; after
the pandemic it accounted for 25--30\%. We therefore compare mothers and childless women across a
pre-period of 2017--2019 and a post-period of 2021--2023, excluding 2020 as a transitional year of
emergency, ad hoc arrangements that reflect improvisation rather than the settled remote-work
practices whose effect we want to measure. (A second, practical reason is that no 2020 CBS extract
exists for this project at all; Section~\ref{sec:data} discusses what that implies for the
exposure index.) The ``post'' dummy captures this structural shift toward remote work.

The core estimator is an individual-level DiD of the form
$Y_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \Mother_i + \beta_2 \Post_t + \beta_3 (\Mother_i \times \Post_t) +
X'_{it}\gamma + \varepsilon_{it}$, where $\beta_3$ is the differential post-shift change for
mothers. To test whether remote work is the \emph{mechanism}, we extend this to a
triple-differences (DDD) model that interacts $\Mother \times \Post$ with an occupation's WFH
exposure, so that the parameter of interest is whether the narrowing of the penalty is larger in
jobs that can actually be done from home.

Our findings are as follows. On the extensive margin there is nothing to see and, more
importantly, nothing the design could have seen: the employment DiD is a precise zero, and the
employment DDD has a minimum detectable effect roughly 8--10 times its point estimate. On the
intensive margin, mothers' hours did not rise on average either: the plain $\Mother \times \Post$
term is $0.23$ (SE $0.18$), a reasonably precise null that rules out average gains above about half
an hour a week. The result is that there is no average effect to decompose --- the entire effect
lives in the interaction with occupational WFH exposure, $3.22$ additional weekly hours per unit of
exposure ($p<0.01$), while the implied change for mothers at zero exposure is if anything negative.
This estimate survives a selection correction, two age-balance robustness specifications, and a
gender placebo: fathers show no positive exposure gradient, their triple interaction being
negative and significant only at the $10\%$ level. We are careful about what the placebo does and
does not establish on the plain DiD, where fathers move \emph{more} than mothers do; we return to
this in Section~\ref{sec:res-subgroup}.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:lit} places the question in the
literature. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data, the sample, and the construction of the WFH
exposure measures. Section~\ref{sec:descriptives} describes the analysis sample and shows both
margins and the exposure measure before any model is imposed on them, and presents the commuting
evidence bearing on the mechanism.
Section~\ref{sec:strategy} states the hypotheses and sets out the DiD and DDD specifications and
the selection correction. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, moving from the extensive to
the intensive margin. Sections~\ref{sec:discussion}--\ref{sec:limitations} interpret the findings
and state their limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Literature Review}\label{sec:lit}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{The motherhood penalty.}
The child penalty is one of the most robust facts in labor economics. \citet{kleven2019denmark}
use Danish administrative data and an event-study design around the birth of the first child to
show that women's earnings fall by roughly 20\% in the long run relative to men's, with the gap
fueled by reduced work hours, exit from the labor market, and moves into family-friendly,
lower-paying jobs. \citet{kleven2019countries} extend the event-study approach across countries and
find that the qualitative pattern is universal while its magnitude varies sharply across
countries, and argue that differences in gender norms, rather than in family policy, are the more
likely explanation. The Danish study matters here in particular because it identifies
\emph{hours} as one of the channels through which the penalty operates --- which is why an
intervention that changes the time cost of combining work and care could plausibly move the
intensive margin even where it does not move employment.

\paragraph{Temporal flexibility as the mechanism.}
\citet{goldin2014} argues that the last chapter of gender convergence turns on the structure of
jobs rather than on human capital: occupations that reward long, particular, and inflexible hours
--- where the marginal return to being physically present at a specific time is high --- carry the
largest gender gaps, and the gap would shrink if firms did not disproportionately reward temporal
inflexibility. Workers who need temporal flexibility --- disproportionately mothers --- pay a wage
penalty for it. This framework generates a sharp, testable prediction: an exogenous change that
lowers the cost of supplying flexibility should differentially help mothers, either by raising
their employment probability, their hours, or both. Remote work is, in this reading, precisely
such a change in temporal flexibility. It
lowers commute friction, allows a virtual presence in demanding professions, and may reduce the
stigma attached to a mother's physical availability. It may also cut the other way, through lower
visibility and slower advancement. Which effect dominates is an empirical question, and Goldin's
framework tells us where to look: in occupations where the work \emph{can} be done remotely.

\paragraph{Does remote work narrow the gap in practice?}
The evidence on whether flexible-work arrangements realize this potential is mixed. In a field
experiment on Italian white-collar workers, \citet{angelici2024} find that randomly assigned
flexible (``smart'') working arrangements shifted the division of household tasks. Women's
reported well-being also increased, suggestive of a genuine flexibility benefit. Real-time survey
evidence from the acute pandemic period tells a more ambiguous story. \citet{adamsprassl2020} show
that WFH-feasible jobs were substantially better shielded from the 2020 employment and earnings
shock. Women in WFH-feasible jobs, however, simultaneously absorbed a larger share of the
additional childcare burden created by school and daycare closures. The same shock that protected
employment could, in principle, degrade the quality of that employment for mothers specifically.
\citet{alon2020} formalize the macro logic for why COVID-19-type shocks hit mothers asymmetrically
(sector composition plus school closures). They do not take a stand on whether WFH exposure
specifically offsets or compounds this asymmetry. This paper is designed to adjudicate empirically
between these two directions using post-acute-period (2021--2023) data, rather than the
initial-shock period that most of this literature studies.

\paragraph{Measuring who can work from home.}
\citet{dingel2020} classify occupations by whether their tasks can be performed entirely at home,
using O*NET task content, and find that 37\% of US jobs can be done from home. Their occupational
classification, mapped to the two-digit ISCO-08 codes in the CBS data, is the starting point for
our WFH-exposure index. As Section~\ref{sec:data} explains, however, a US task-based measure
misclassifies occupations where Israeli institutional practice diverged from what the tasks would
predict, and we therefore calibrate it against realized Israeli behavior. For the Israeli labor
market specifically, \citet{yaish2021} document that the 2020 lockdown reallocated paid and unpaid
labor within dual-earner households in gender-unequal ways. \citet{madhala2020} provide a
descriptive occupation-level baseline of WFH feasibility among Israeli workers.
\citet{buzaglo2023} shows that within-firm sorting, not occupation choice alone, is a first-order
driver of the Israeli gender wage gap. This is a reminder that any occupation-based exposure
measure captures only part of the relevant heterogeneity.

\paragraph{Remote work and the motherhood penalty.}
The paper closest to ours is \citet{harrington2025}, who show for the United States that the rise
of WFH raised mothers' employment relative to other women's, that this is driven by fields with
high returns to hours and inflexible demands on time, and that women who could work from home
before childbirth are less likely to exit the workforce. We take their question to Israel. Our
contribution is threefold: a different country with a distinctive combination of high fertility
and high productivity; a different and, in the child-penalty literature, central margin --- hours
conditional on working --- with an explicit correction for the selection problem that
conditioning on employment creates; and an exposure measure calibrated to Israeli practice rather
than imported from US task content. Because the data span both pre- and multiple post-COVID years,
they also allow separate identification of extensive- and intensive-margin responses --- a
distinction the WFH-and-gender literature above largely does not draw. And because the exposure
measure is built from pre-determined occupational content rather than realized WFH status, it
avoids the reverse-causality problem inherent in treating self-reported WFH as an explanatory
variable for hours.

\paragraph{Identification.}
The triple-differences estimator we use for the mechanism test is analyzed formally by
\citet{olden2022}, who clarify that the DDD does not require two parallel-trends assumptions but
rather a single assumption that the \emph{difference} between the two DiDs would have evolved
in parallel absent treatment. Section~\ref{sec:strategy} states the identifying assumptions in
those terms.

\section{Data and Institutional Context}\label{sec:data}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Source and sample}

The data are individual-level microdata from the Israeli Central Bureau of Statistics Labor Force
Survey for the survey years 2017, 2018, 2019, 2021, 2022, and 2023. The analysis sample is women
aged 25--59 (CBS age-group codes 3--7). A woman is coded as a \emph{mother} if she has any child
under 17 in the household, and as childless otherwise; the treatment group is mothers and the
control group is childless women. \emph{Post} equals one for survey years 2021 and later.

Two outcomes are studied. The primary outcome is \emph{usual weekly work hours}, a continuous
variable constructed from the CBS binned hours item by assigning each bin its median and imputing
irregular-hours codes from observed medians within the same period; it is defined for women who are
employed \emph{and} worked in the survey's reference week.

That second restriction is not cosmetic, and it is worth stating plainly because it changes the
estimand. In the 2017 file, respondents who were employed but absent from work in the reference
week had their usual hours recorded as zero --- the code CBS otherwise uses for ``no usual hours''
--- whereas from 2018 onward the same respondents were assigned a real usual-hours value. Zero is a
defensible entry for \emph{actual} hours in a week not worked; it is not a usual-hours value, and
left uncorrected it enters the pre-period as roughly $5{,}200$ spurious zeros. Because absence from
the reference week is itself more common among mothers ($12.4\%$ of employed mothers against
$7.3\%$ of childless women), those zeros do not fall evenly across the comparison this paper makes.
We therefore define hours on the reference-week workers in \emph{every} year, which costs about
$10\%$ of each year's employed sample and makes the hours population identical across years. The
estimand is correspondingly narrower: usual hours among women who were working, not among all women
who held a job. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} returns to what this does and does not fix.

The secondary outcome is a binary employment indicator, equal to one if the
respondent reports being employed and zero otherwise (unemployed and not-in-labor-force are both
zero). Among women working in the reference week the pooled mean of weekly hours is $39.18$
($n = 258{,}175$) and the
pooled employment rate is $0.7737$ ($n = 372{,}741$).

All regressions include the same set of categorical controls: marital status, religion,
age group, district of residence, and highest educational attainment (six groups). Age enters as
the categorical CBS age-group code because the extract contains no continuous age or birth-year
variable. CBS survey weights are deliberately not applied in any outcome regression; all reported
estimates are unweighted estimates on the analysis sample rather than population-representative
statistics (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Why 2017--2019 versus 2021--2023}

The window is designed around the pandemic's role in making WFH a mainstream practice: working
from home rose from about 5\% of workdays before 2020 to 25--30\% afterward. The post-period is
therefore 2021--2023 and the pre-period 2017--2019. The year 2020 is excluded as a transitional
year. There is also a data-driven reason: no 2020 CBS extract was acquired for this project, so
2020 could not be included even in principle. The consequence that matters for the design is
discussed next.

\subsection{Constructing WFH exposure}\label{sec:exposure}

Since realized WFH status is itself a labor-market outcome --- jointly determined with employment
and hours --- causal designs need a measure of WFH \emph{exposure} that is not chosen by the
individual whose hours are being modeled. The mechanism test therefore requires an
occupation-level measure of how exposed each job is to remote work. We build it in three steps,
each producing a measure that is reported.

\emph{External.} The \citet{dingel2020} teleworkability score, via an O*NET/SOC-to-ISCO crosswalk,
at the two-digit ISCO-08 level. This is pre-period by construction and immune to Israel's own
COVID-era behavior, but it is a US task-based measure and misclassifies occupations where Israeli
institutional practice diverged: teaching (ISCO 23) scores $0.966$ on the external index although
Israeli schools stayed in person by Ministry of Education policy.

\emph{Calibrated (primary).} We correct the external score only where the gap between it and the
realized 2022--23 Israeli WFH share is large (greater than $0.5$) \emph{and} statistically
distinguishable from sampling noise, using a one-sided cluster-robust test at the 95\% level. Ten of
the 40 two-digit occupations are swapped to their realized Israeli values. The corrections are
concrete and checkable: teaching falls from $0.966$ to $0.069$, clerical support (ISCO 41) from
$1.000$ to $0.098$, while ICT professionals (ISCO 25) go from $1.000$ to $0.360$. Because it draws
on 2022--23 realized data, which sit inside the post-period, the calibrated measure is a documented
compromise rather than a fully pre-treatment index; the exposure measures are built from a
population that includes men as well as the women in the analysis sample, so that an occupation's
score is not constructed from exactly the people who enter the regression.

\emph{Realized.} The realized Israeli WFH share by occupation, anchored to 2021 with a minimum of
200 observations per occupation. This is post-treatment by construction and is reported as a
robustness check.

The original research plan specified anchoring the realized index to the 2020 WFH variable. Because
no 2020 extract exists, the index is anchored to 2021 instead. This is a disclosed deviation with a
stated justification --- 2021 already flows through the validated pipeline, and a measure taken
during 2020's emergency disruption might reflect improvisation rather than each occupation's
settled remote-work potential --- and a stated downside: by 2021 occupations had had a year to
adapt, and if adaptation was uneven a 2021-based index could rank occupations differently than a
2020-based one would have. We return to this in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

For the employment-outcome DDD, occupation is undefined for the non-employed, so an
occupation-level regressor would condition the outcome on itself. That specification therefore
uses a fourth measure: a pre-period (2017--2019) shift-share index by demographic cell (sex, age
group, education, district, marital status, religion, and continent of birth --- 8,884 cells),
equal to the weighted mean of the calibrated occupation score over the cell's pre-period
occupational composition. It is defined for employed and non-employed women alike. The two DDDs
thus use \emph{different} exposure measures, and we are careful never to compare their
coefficients as if they were on a common scale.

\section{Descriptive Statistics}\label{sec:descriptives}
%======================================================================

This section sets out what the analysis sample looks like before any model is imposed on it. The
order is deliberate: the summary table, then the primary (hours) margin, then the exposure measure
that identifies the triple difference, then the commuting evidence bearing on the mechanism, and
finally the secondary (employment) margin. Nothing here is adjusted for the controls listed in
Section~\ref{sec:data}, and nothing here carries an identifying assumption; the estimates begin in
Section~\ref{sec:results}.

One convention applies throughout. All standard errors and confidence intervals in this section are
clustered by individual, as they are in Section~\ref{sec:results}. This matters more than it may
appear: the survey is a rotating panel in which a respondent is observed roughly four times overall
and two to three times within a single year, so the independent-observations formulas normally used
for a cell mean or a difference of proportions understate these standard errors by a factor of
about $1.8$ to $2$. The point estimates are ordinary cell arithmetic --- a difference of means is
still a difference of means --- but the intervals around them are not.

\subsection{The raw hours gap}\label{sec:desc-hours}

Before any model, the primary outcome in four cell means. Mothers work $38.54$ hours per week
before 2021 and $38.84$ after, a gain of $0.31$ hours. Childless women move from $40.04$ to
$40.08$, a gain of $0.04$. The difference between those two changes is $0.266$ hours, and it is the
entire difference-in-differences of equation~\eqref{eq:did} computed without a single control
variable.

This matters for how the later estimate should be read. The regression DiD of Table~\ref{tab:hours}
is $0.2280$ (SE $0.1809$), within four hundredths of an hour of the raw figure and with an almost
identical standard error. What the controls do not do, in other words, is manufacture or conceal
an average effect: the near-absence of one is already present in the arithmetic of four averages,
and neither version is distinguishable from zero.

Note also that mothers remain well below childless women in both periods, and that whatever
movement there is would close only a small fraction of that gap even if one took it at face value.
The average mother's hours are not what moves in this paper; Section~\ref{sec:desc-exposure} shows
what does.

\subsection{Hours year by year}\label{sec:desc-pretrend}

Pooling the pre-period into a single average hides whether the two groups were on a common
trajectory before 2021, so Figure~\ref{fig:hours-by-year} unpools it. The lower panel plots the raw
mother-minus-non-mother hours gap in each survey year. The pre-period gap is $-1.37$ hours in 2017,
$-1.48$ in 2018 and $-1.67$ in 2019: three years within about a third of an hour of each other, and
each within the others' confidence bounds. Whatever else is true of this design, the groups were
not diverging before treatment.

This is the descriptive counterpart of the formal test in Table~\ref{tab:pretrend}, which does not
reject ($F(2, 63{,}198) = 0.949$, $p = 0.387$). It is worth being explicit that this was not always
so. Before the hours population was harmonized on reference-week work
(Section~\ref{sec:data}), the 2017 gap read $-3.30$ and the pre-trend test rejected decisively ---
an artifact of the spurious zeros described there, which fell disproportionately on mothers and so
manufactured exactly the kind of pre-existing divergence a reader should worry about.

The same panel shows the post-period path: the gap is $-1.37$ in 2021, widens slightly to $-1.53$
in 2022, and then narrows to $-0.80$ in 2023, the smallest value in the sample. The movement is
not monotone, and at these standard errors the three post-period years are not individually
distinguishable from one another; it is the event study of Section~\ref{sec:res-hours}, not this
raw series, that supports the gradual-adjustment reading in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Exposure and the commuting channel}\label{sec:desc-exposure}

The triple difference asks whether hours respond where work can be done from home.
Figure~\ref{fig:dose-response} puts that question to the data directly, by computing the same
difference-in-differences as Section~\ref{sec:desc-hours} separately within each quartile of
occupational WFH exposure. The answer is not that the response is larger at the top; it is that
there is no response anywhere else. The bottom three quartiles return $-0.32$, $0.27$ and $-0.02$
hours, none of them distinguishable from zero. The top quartile returns $1.55$ hours
(95\% CI $[0.74, 2.36]$).

Two things follow. First, this is why Section~\ref{sec:desc-hours}'s average is near zero: it is a
weighted average of three nulls and one substantial positive difference, and the three nulls
dominate by construction. The decomposition is approximate rather than exact --- these quartiles
additionally require a matched occupation code, so they rest on $251{,}968$ rows against
Section~\ref{sec:desc-hours}'s $258{,}175$, and the quartile-weighted average is $0.29$ rather than
$0.27$. Second, the dose-response that the DDD coefficient of $3.224$ summarises as a single slope
is visible without any regression --- but it is emphatically not linear in exposure, and the
identifying contrast is a comparison of the most teleworkable quartile against the rest rather than
a smooth gradient. The quartile boundaries make the same point: the bottom
three quartiles span exposure values of $0.00$ to $0.30$, while the top quartile spans $0.30$ to
$0.75$. Most of the dispersion in the regressor lives in one tail, which is worth bearing in mind
when reading a coefficient expressed per unit of exposure.

Commuting patterns give a descriptive reason to think this channel is the operative one. Among
women aged 25--59 with a recorded locality of work --- a population \emph{broader} than the
reference-week workers the hours figures use, 56,323 against 47,728 in 2017 --- mothers were the
more mobile group in every year, working outside their locality of residence $48.2\%$ of the time
in 2019 against $46.1\%$ of childless women, which is not what a simple story about mothers being
tied to home would predict. Their commuting was also the less disturbed by the pandemic: the share
fell $1.5$ percentage points for mothers in 2021 against $3.9$ for childless women, a difference of
$2.39$ points (SE $1.17$, clustered by individual), and both series had recovered to their 2019
levels by 2023. This is consistent with the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, in which
remote work relaxes a time constraint for women who were already commuting, rather than drawing a
previously home-bound group into work --- though it is descriptive evidence on that mechanism
rather than a test of it, since no specification in this paper uses locality of work as an outcome.

\subsection{The extensive margin by child age}\label{sec:desc-childage}

Employment among mothers traces a shallow inverted U in the age of the youngest child, from
$75.9\%$ at ages 0--1 to a peak of $80.2\%$ at ages 5--9 and back to $76.7\%$ at ages 15--17. The
trough-to-peak range is about four percentage points, and even the trough sits above the $75.7\%$
employment rate of childless women in Table~\ref{tab:descriptives}. The extensive margin is where
this sample has the least room to move, which is part of why hours, and not employment, is this
paper's primary outcome.\footnote{Splitting the same profile by period rather than pooling it, the
pre- and post-2021 profiles are within half a percentage point of each other where the youngest
child is under five --- $0.3$ and $0.5$ points in the 0--1 and 2--4 bins --- and around four points
apart in each of the three older bins. There is no comparison group in that comparison, so the
distance is a difference in levels within one group and not an estimate of anything, and in
particular not a difference-in-differences. The identified extensive-margin result is the DiD of
Table~\ref{tab:emp-did}.}

That profile is partly compositional. Regressing employment on the youngest-child age bin and the
standard controls and averaging the predictions over the sample, the adjusted profile does not turn
down after age 5--9: it rises monotonically to $81.8\%$ in the 15--17 bin against a raw $76.7\%$,
while the 0--1 bin falls to $72.9\%$ against a raw $75.9\%$. Mothers whose youngest child is nearly
grown are themselves older and differently educated, and once that is accounted for their
employment rate is the highest in the sample rather than among the lowest. These are employment
\emph{levels} among mothers with no comparison group, so the raw-to-adjusted distance is a
statement about composition within one group, not an estimate of anything. It should not be read
against the employment DiD of Table~\ref{tab:emp-did}, which is estimated relative to childless
women and with controls, and which is a precise null.

\section{Empirical Strategy}\label{sec:strategy}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Hypotheses}

Two hypotheses follow directly from the flexibility framework of \citet{goldin2014} and the mixed
empirical record reviewed above. \textbf{H1 (intensive margin):} WFH exposure raises mothers'
hours conditional on working relative to childless women after 2021, if flexibility relaxes a
binding time constraint while the participation margin remains the more costly one to adjust.
\textbf{H2 (extensive margin):} occupational WFH exposure raises mothers' employment probability
relative to other women after 2021, if flexibility relaxes a binding participation constraint. H1
is the paper's primary hypothesis; H2 is tested but, for the power reasons documented below, is
secondary.

\subsection{Difference-in-differences}

For outcome $Y_{it}$ (employment, or weekly hours conditional on working that week) we estimate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:did}
Y_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \Mother_i + \beta_2 \Post_t + \beta_3 (\Mother_i \times \Post_t)
+ X'_{it}\gamma + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
where $X_{it}$ is the control vector described above and standard errors are clustered by
individual identifier. $\beta_3$ is the DiD estimator: the differential change in the outcome for
mothers relative to childless women after the shift to remote work. For the hours specification a
positive $\beta_3$ indicates a narrowing of the penalty on the intensive margin; for employment it
indicates a narrowing on the extensive margin. The employment DiD is also estimated separately for
Jewish and Arab women.

\subsection{Triple differences}

To test whether the narrowing operates through remote work, we interact the DiD with occupational
WFH exposure:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ddd}
Y_{it} = \alpha + \Mother_i \times \Post_t \times \WFH_{j(i)} \text{ (all lower-order terms)}
+ X'_{it}\gamma + \varepsilon_{it}.
\end{equation}
The coefficient on the triple interaction asks whether the post-shift change for mothers relative
to non-mothers is larger in more exposed occupations. Following \citet{olden2022}, the identifying
assumption is that the mother/non-mother gap in high- versus low-exposure jobs would have evolved
in parallel absent the shift to remote work. For the hours DDD, $\WFH_j$ is the calibrated
occupation-level score of Section~\ref{sec:exposure}, the sample is employed women matched to an
occupation, and standard errors are clustered by two-digit occupation --- the level at which the
regressor varies, so that individual-level clustering would understate the standard error. For the
employment DDD, $\WFH$ is the cell-based shift-share index, estimated on the full sample of
employed and non-employed women, with a $\Mother \times \text{age group}$ interaction added to
correct a documented pre-period age imbalance between mothers and non-mothers, in two variants:
additive controls (Spec~1) and fully interacted age-by-education-by-district cell fixed effects
(Spec~2). Standard errors are clustered on the age-by-education-by-district cell (about 210
clusters). We also report the exposure-specific minimum detectable effect,
$\mathrm{MDE} = \mathrm{SE} \times (z_{0.975} + z_{0.80}) = 2.8016 \times \mathrm{SE}$, so that a
null coefficient can be read as ``genuinely null'' or ``underpowered.''

\subsection{Selection into employment: Lee bounds}

Hours are observed only for women who are working, and whether a woman is working is itself an
outcome of the same treatment. If remote work differentially pulls marginal mothers into work after
2021, the post-period sample of working mothers is compositionally different from the pre-period one
for reasons unrelated to hours choices, biasing $\beta_3$ in the hours equation in a direction that
is not signable a priori. We address this with trimming bounds in the spirit of \citet{lee2009},
adapted to the two-by-two design. Let $s_{ab} = \Pr(\text{hours observed} \mid \Mother=a, \Post=b)$
--- that is, the probability of being employed \emph{and} working in the reference week, which is
the sample the outcome is measured on rather than the employed population as a whole.
The counterfactual below assumes parallel trends \emph{in selection}. For post-period mothers it is
$s^*_{11} = s_{10} + (s_{01} - s_{00})$. If the observed $s_{11}$ exceeds $s^*_{11}$, the excess
share $p = 1 - s^*_{11}/s_{11}$ is trimmed from the $(\Mother=1,\Post=1)$ cell's hours
distribution --- from the top to obtain a lower bound on $\beta_3$, from the bottom to obtain an
upper bound --- and equation~\eqref{eq:did} is re-estimated on each trimmed sample. For the DDD we
generalize this construction: the selection counterfactual is computed separately within each
quartile of the cell-based exposure index (which, unlike the occupation-level index, is observable
for the non-employed), each quartile's post-period mothers are trimmed by that quartile's own
excess share, the trimmed subsets are recombined, and equation~\eqref{eq:ddd} is refit with the
occupation-level regressor. Two different exposure measures thus serve two different roles.
Because the parameter is only partially identified, we report, alongside each bound's own
cluster-robust confidence interval, the \citet{imbens2004} confidence interval for the identified
set, which accounts jointly for the sampling uncertainty of both endpoints. The construction
handles only \emph{excess} selection in the treated cell, and assumes monotone selection; both
limitations are stated in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Diagnostics and placebo}

We estimate event-study versions of equation~\eqref{eq:did} with $\Mother \times$ year
interactions (reference year 2019) for both outcomes and test the pre-period coefficients jointly.
Because those test the difference-in-differences assumption rather than the triple-differences one,
we also estimate the event-study version of equation~\eqref{eq:ddd} itself --- $\Post$ replaced by
survey-year interactions at every level, so that $\Mother \times$ year $\times \WFH$ is recovered
separately for each year --- and apply the same joint test to its pre-period coefficients.
We re-estimate the hours DDD with an added $\Mother \times$ age-group interaction and with
pre-period age-group raking weights, in response to the age imbalance noted above; with the
external and realized exposure indices in place of the calibrated one; and, as a gender placebo,
on the male subsample with ``mother'' read as ``father,'' comparing the male and female
coefficients with two-sample $z$-tests (valid without a covariance correction because the samples
are disjoint).

\subsection{Extensive margin: the employment DDD is underpowered}\label{sec:res-emp-ddd}

Table~\ref{tab:emp-ddd} reports the employment DDD with the cell-based exposure index. The triple
interaction is $-0.0257$ (SE $0.0725$) with additive controls and $-0.0194$ (SE $0.0722$) with
interacted cell fixed effects --- small, negative, and nowhere near significant. The question is
whether this is evidence of no mechanism or evidence of nothing. The minimum detectable effect
answers it: at $\alpha = 0.05$ and 80\% power, the design could detect a triple interaction no
smaller than $0.2031$ (Spec~1) or $0.2022$ (Spec~2), roughly 8--10 times the point estimates.
That ratio is the cleanest statement of the problem, because it is invariant to how the regressor
is scaled: the design cannot detect an effect the size of the one it estimates.

Putting the coefficient on an interpretable scale requires care, because it is an effect per
\emph{unit} of exposure and the cell-based index never moves a unit: on the estimation sample its
standard deviation is $0.0706$ and its interquartile range $0.0766$. A one-standard-deviation
increase in exposure would therefore have to shift mothers' relative post-2021 employment
probability by $1.43$ percentage points, or $1.85\%$ of the baseline employment rate of $0.7737$,
before this design could see it. For comparison, \citet{harrington2025} report roughly $0.78$
percentage points per 10\% rise in WFH, so the design is underpowered against the closest
available benchmark by a factor of about two to three. The null here is therefore
\emph{uninformative}: the employment DDD cannot be read as evidence for or against a WFH-mediated
employment effect.

The shortfall is structural rather than a specification choice, and we report that having tried to
close it. Clustering on the exposure cell rather than the coarser fixed-effect cell changes the
standard error from $0.0725$ to $0.0711$; estimating the combined margin instead, with hours coded
zero for the non-employed, leaves the triple interaction at less than half its own minimum
detectable effect; and two successive refinements of the exposure-cell partition had already cut
the detectable effect from $51\%$ of baseline to the present figure. A binary outcome and a
regressor with a standard deviation of $0.07$ do not support a tighter design. The
$\Post \times \WFH$ main effect, by contrast, is negative and significant in both specifications
($-0.1555$\sym{**} and $-0.1516$\sym{**}). This indicates a general post-2021 employment decline in
high-exposure cells that is unrelated to motherhood specifically.

This is not for want of a working exposure measure. The cell-based index strongly predicts realized
post-period WFH: a first-stage regression of the reference-week WFH indicator on exposure in the
post-period yields $0.6557$\sym{***} (SE $0.0954$, $n = 110{,}051$), stable across 2021--2023
(interactions with 2022 and 2023 of $0.0118$ and $0.0379$, the latter marginal). Nor is it a
specification failure: the exposure cells are deliberately finer than the fixed-effect cells, so
the regressor retains within-cell variation ($R^2$ of exposure on the fixed-effect variables
$0.285$, VIF $1.40$). An earlier version of the design, in which exposure was built on exactly the
regression's own control variables, had an MDE of 51\% of baseline; successive refinements of the
cell definition halved it to the current 26\%, and cell-level aggregation was confirmed to add no
power. The remaining limitation is structural: exposure assigned at the demographic-cell level
simply does not vary enough. This is what motivates the intensive-margin analysis, where
occupation-level exposure is available.

\paragraph{The hours DiD: a null.}
Column~(1) of Table~\ref{tab:hours} reports equation~\eqref{eq:did} for weekly hours among women
working in the reference week. Mothers work $1.739$ fewer hours per week than childless women
($\Mother = -1.739$\sym{***}), the intensive-margin penalty itself. That penalty did not
measurably change after the shift to remote work: $\Mother \times \Post = 0.2280$ (SE $0.1809$),
$t = 1.26$, $N = 251{,}857$. The raw cell means of Section~\ref{sec:desc-hours} agree on magnitude:
the unadjusted difference-in-differences is $0.266$, so the controls move the estimate by less than
four hundredths of an hour. They do not agree on precision --- the unclustered standard error is
$0.098$, roughly half the clustered one --- which is why the raw arithmetic should not be read as
evidence of a significant increase.

Following the standard set in Section~\ref{sec:res-emp-did}, it is worth saying what kind of null
this is. At this standard error the design's minimum detectable effect is
$2.8016 \times 0.1809 \approx 0.51$ hours, so the estimate rules out average gains much above half
an hour a week. This is a reasonably precise null, not an uninformative one --- a distinction that
matters here for the same reason it mattered on the extensive margin.

Selection into the estimation sample does not change that reading. The probability that hours are
observed for post-period mothers ($0.6964$) exceeds the parallel-trends-in-selection counterfactual
($s^*_{11} = 0.6786 + (0.7058 - 0.7010) = 0.6833$), so there is excess selection to trim --- about
1.9\% of the cell.\footnote{Derived from the selection rates reported in the note to
Table~\ref{tab:lee-did} ($1 - 0.6833/0.6964$), not directly exported by the pipeline.}
Table~\ref{tab:lee-did} reports the resulting Lee bounds: the lower bound is $-0.5166$
(SE $0.1781$) and the upper bound $0.8017$ (SE $0.1789$), with an Imbens--Manski 95\% confidence
interval for the identified set of $[-0.810, 1.096]$. The correction widens the interval around a
null rather than overturning a finding. We therefore do not treat the plain hours DiD as evidence
of an average effect in either direction, and nothing that follows rests on it.

\paragraph{The hours DDD: the paper's headline result.}
Column~(2) of Table~\ref{tab:hours} interacts the DiD with the calibrated occupation-level
exposure index. The triple interaction is $3.224$\sym{**} (SE $1.022$, $N = 246{,}326$): among
mothers who are working, those in more WFH-exposed occupations saw a larger post-2021 increase in
weekly hours relative to childless women in the same occupations --- about $3.2$ additional hours
per unit of exposure, where exposure runs from zero to one.

The column's second row is as informative as its first. At zero exposure --- an occupation that
cannot be done from home at all --- the implied change for mothers is $\Mother \times \Post =
-0.5385$ (SE $0.3364$), if anything negative. Set against the near-zero average effect of
column~(1), the reading is not that a broad gain is \emph{larger} in teleworkable jobs. It is that
outside teleworkable jobs there is no gain to speak of, and the entire effect is the exposure
gradient. That is a sharper claim than the one a significant average DiD would have supported, and
it is the claim Figure~\ref{fig:dose-response} shows directly: three quartiles at zero and one
well above it.

The estimate remains inside the design's power, though by less of a margin than the extensive
margin's failure might suggest is easy: the minimum detectable effect is $2.8640$ hours
($7.3\%$ of mean weekly hours, or $88.8\%$ of the point estimate). The contrast with the employment
DDD --- whose MDE is eight to ten times \emph{its} point estimate --- still holds, but this design
detects an effect of the size observed without a great deal of room to spare.

\paragraph{Pre-trends for the triple difference.}
The pre-trend tests of Table~\ref{tab:pretrend} license equation~\eqref{eq:did}, not
equation~\eqref{eq:ddd}. They ask whether mothers and childless women trended together; the triple
difference requires the stricter condition that the mother/non-mother gap trended together
\emph{across levels of WFH exposure}. These are not the same assumption, and the
difference-in-differences version is not the conservative one: because it averages over exposure, a
pre-period divergence correlated with exposure but netting to zero across occupations would leave
the test of Table~\ref{tab:pretrend} untouched while invalidating the headline estimate. We
therefore estimate the event-study analogue of equation~\eqref{eq:ddd} --- replacing $\Post$ with a
full set of survey-year interactions at every level of the interaction, so that the triple
difference is recovered separately for each year against 2019 --- on the same sample, the same
exposure index, and the same occupation-level clustering as column~(2) of Table~\ref{tab:hours}.

Table~\ref{tab:pretrend-ddd} and Figure~\ref{fig:pretrend-ddd} report the result, and it is
supportive. Neither pre-period coefficient is close to significant, and neither is far from zero in
level: $0.5866$ in 2017 and $0.3134$ in 2018, against post-period estimates five to thirteen times
larger. The joint Wald test does not reject, and does not come near rejecting
($F(2, 39) = 0.041$, $p = 0.960$). The post-period path is flat, then steps up in 2021 and rises:
$3.011$\sym{*}, $3.545$\sym{**}, $4.020$\sym{**}, all three individually significant and straddling
the pooled $3.224$ of Table~\ref{tab:hours}, which is what a treatment beginning in 2021 looks like
rather than a pre-existing trend carried through the sample.

One caveat belongs with this test rather than in a footnote. Clustering at the level the exposure
regressor actually varies at leaves forty occupation clusters, so the pre-period standard errors
($2.2367$ and $1.7449$) are of the same order as the effect being tested. Each pre-period confidence
interval therefore contains values larger in magnitude than $3.224$: the test is consistent with
parallel trends and shows no sign of a violation, but it does not have the power to rule out a
pre-trend as large as the estimate itself. This is the same small-cluster limitation the headline
estimate carries, and we read it as supporting evidence rather than as a closed question.

\paragraph{Robustness.}
Table~\ref{tab:robust} collects the remaining checks. With the raw \citet{dingel2020} external
index in place of the calibrated one, the triple interaction is $0.6720$ (SE $0.8838$), positive
but insignificant; with the realized 2021-anchored index it is $5.195$\sym{*} (SE $2.363$). The
direction is consistent across all three measures but the magnitude and significance are not, and
we describe the result as directionally rather than point-estimate robust to the exposure measure.
The calibrated measure is the one purpose-built to correct the external index's known
misclassification of Israeli occupations, and we lead with it. Adding a $\Mother \times$ age-group
interaction leaves the estimate essentially unchanged ($3.205$\sym{**}, SE $1.055$); reweighting
the sample to balance pre-period age groups between mothers and non-mothers attenuates it by about
19\% to $2.597$\sym{*} (SE $1.050$) --- still significant, though at the $5\%$ level rather than
the $1\%$, and close enough to the design's minimum detectable effect of $2.864$ that we would not
want to rest the result on this specification alone. The age imbalance being corrected is real and
tracks exposure --- the pre-period mother/non-mother gap in the age-group code is $-0.974$ in the
lowest exposure quartile ($t = -94.0$), $-0.735$ in the second, $-0.169$ in the third, and $+0.057$
in the highest --- so the reweighted attenuation is worth reporting, and it is the largest single
move any check here produces. An incidental finding of the age-interacted specification is a
rising age profile in the hours penalty itself, independent of exposure ($\Mother \times$ age
group 4: $-2.266$\sym{***}; group 5: $-1.150$\sym{*}; groups 6 and 7 positive but not significant
at conventional levels).

\subsection{Heterogeneity by population group and the gender placebo}\label{sec:res-subgroup}

Table~\ref{tab:subgroup} re-estimates the hours DiD and DDD separately for Jewish and Arab women,
and on men as a placebo. For Jewish women the pattern is the pooled pattern: the DiD is $0.3021$
(SE $0.2027$), indistinguishable from zero, while the DDD is $2.8465$\sym{**} (SE $0.9125$), with a
confidence interval that excludes zero. For Arab women the DiD is $-0.0724$ (SE $0.4785$), also
indistinguishable from zero --- and Arab women's hours fell in the post-period overall
($\Post = -2.162$\sym{***}) --- while the DDD is $5.5069$\sym{*} (SE $2.6741$, 95\% CI
$[0.2657, 10.7481]$), nominally significant and nearly twice the Jewish point estimate. Neither
group shows an average effect; both show an exposure gradient.

We do not read the larger Arab estimate as evidence that the mechanism is stronger for Arab women,
for two reasons. First, the two coefficients are not statistically distinguishable. The Arab
subsample is about nine times smaller than the Jewish one ($24{,}307$ versus $209{,}727$ occupation-matched
women with observed hours), the Arab standard error is nearly three times the Jewish one, and a
two-sample $z$-test on the DDD term gives
$z = (5.5069 - 2.8465)/\sqrt{2.6741^2 + 0.9125^2} = 0.942$, $p = 0.346$ --- the apparent gap is well
within what the Arab subsample's own sampling noise can produce. (The same test on the DiD gives
$z = -0.720$, $p = 0.471$.) Second, there is a plausible mechanical cause for the imprecision:
Arab women's employment in Israel is concentrated in a narrow set of occupations, disproportionately
education and health-aide roles, so that comparatively few two-digit occupations carry enough
Arab respondents to inform an occupation-specific comparison. With few occupations carrying real
information, a small number of high-leverage occupations can dominate the estimate. The Arab result is therefore best
described as imprecisely estimated and statistically indistinguishable from the Jewish estimate ---
a small-sample, thin-occupational-coverage fragility rather than a documented heterogeneous
treatment effect. The Jewish-women estimate is the more credible of the two and anchors any
subgroup claim we make.

The gender placebo has to be read separately on the two margins, because it behaves differently on
each. Re-estimating the same models on men, with ``mother'' read as ``father,'' gives a DiD of
$-0.4002$\sym{*} (SE $0.1883$, $N = 261{,}543$) and a DDD of $-1.7620$ (SE $1.0153$,
$N = 251{,}779$), the latter significant only at the $10\%$ level and negative.

On the triple interaction the placebo does what a placebo should, and more. Fathers show no
positive WFH-linked hours response; their point estimate is negative; and the women's and men's
coefficients are statistically distinguishable ($z = 3.461$, $p = 0.0005$). That is a stronger
statement than ``the placebo is insignificant,'' because a macro trend common to all workers cannot
produce a positive exposure gradient for mothers alongside a negative one for fathers. The
mechanism claim rests on this margin.

On the plain DiD the placebo is not merely uninformative but adverse, and we would rather say so
than let a reader discover it. Fathers' hours fall by $0.40$ (SE $0.19$) relative to childless men
--- a \emph{larger} movement, and a more statistically significant one, than the $0.23$ (SE $0.18$)
we estimate for mothers. The two are still distinguishable ($z = 2.406$, $p = 0.016$), but the
difference is driven by the fathers' coefficient rather than the mothers'. A design whose placebo
moves more than its treatment group has not demonstrated a clean average effect on that margin, and
we do not claim one: Section~\ref{sec:res-hours} reads the female DiD as a null, and the placebo is
consistent with that reading rather than against it. What would be troubling is a significant
\emph{positive} placebo on the exposure gradient, and there is none.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
%======================================================================

Among mothers who remain in work, there is no post-2021 hours effect on average. There is a large
one in teleworkable occupations. Those two statements are the paper's result, and it is the
conjunction that makes it informative: a triple-interaction coefficient of about $3.2$ additional
weekly hours per unit of exposure, an implied change at zero exposure that is if anything negative,
and a selection correction the estimate survives. This is consistent with WFH access relaxing a
binding time constraint specifically for mothers in teleworkable jobs --- the \citet{goldin2014}
mechanism --- and it is difficult to reconcile with a general post-pandemic recovery common to all
women, which would have raised hours across occupations rather than only where the work can be done
from home. Fathers show no analogous exposure gradient.

Which occupations are doing the work is concrete. The calibration step moved ten occupations away
from their US task-based scores toward realized Israeli behavior: clerical support and teaching
were corrected sharply downward, because Israeli institutional practice kept them in person
regardless of task content, while ICT professionals remained high on both measures. The
triple interaction is identified off exactly this contrast, which grounds ``exposure'' in checkable
occupational content. That the raw external index yields a positive but insignificant estimate is
itself informative: the mechanism shows up cleanly only once exposure is measured as Israeli jobs
were actually done, not as US task content says they could be.

The timing is also informative. The event study rises gradually from an insignificant 2021
estimate to a significant one in 2023, consistent with WFH adoption and household time
reallocation being a multi-year adjustment rather than an instantaneous response to the 2020--21
shock --- plausible given that occupational WFH norms were themselves still settling over
2021--2023.

Why hours and not employment? The employment DDD is not a failed test of the mechanism; it is an
uninformative one: its minimum detectable effect is roughly eight to ten times its own point
estimate, and about twice to three times the effect the closest study in the literature reports.
The intensive-margin design is well powered because it can use occupation-level exposure, which the
employment design cannot without conditioning its own outcome on itself. Substantively, the two
results are coherent: the effect operates on \emph{how much} mothers in teleworkable jobs work, not
on \emph{whether} they are employed, which is precisely the margin \citet{kleven2019denmark}
identify as a leading channel of the child penalty. This is also where our findings depart from
\citet{harrington2025}: their US evidence is on employment and income, and we cannot confirm or
reject an employment effect in Israel with this design; what we can say is that the intensive
margin responds \emph{to remote-work exposure}, and not otherwise. A purely statistical account is
not mutually exclusive, and on the present results it is the least strained one: the continuous
hours outcome carries strictly more information per observation than a binary employment indicator
and is correspondingly better powered to detect an effect of realistic magnitude, so the contrast
between the two margins may be as much about what each design can see as about what workers did.

We previously read this pattern as evidence that labor supply adjusts on the cheaper margin ---
hours --- before the costlier one of leaving employment, an account for which Israel's furlough
(\textit{Halat}) system offers institutional support. We no longer think the data bear that
reading. A margin-ordering story predicts an average hours response, and there is none; and it says
nothing about why the response we do find is graded by teleworkability. The Goldin time-constraint
mechanism accounts for the exposure gradient directly, and we rest on it. The policy reading is
correspondingly narrower: WFH availability matters for how intensively mothers who already hold
\emph{teleworkable} jobs can work, not for whether mothers in general hold jobs or how much they
work --- which is where our evidence speaks, and where it does not.

The subgroup results are more modest than the point estimates suggest. The Jewish-women estimate
carries the pooled result. The Arab-women estimate is larger but imprecise, statistically
indistinguishable from the Jewish one, and rests on thin occupational coverage; we report it
transparently but do not claim heterogeneity on its basis.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{Parallel trends, and a measurement correction that produced them.}
Joint pre-trend tests do not reject on either margin --- $F(2, 63{,}198) = 0.949$, $p = 0.387$ for
hours and $F(2, 79{,}069) = 0.63$, $p = 0.53$ for employment --- so the identifying assumption is
supported over the full 2017--2019 pre-period rather than a truncated version of it. The triple
difference's own pre-trend test, on the event-study version of equation~\eqref{eq:ddd}
(Table~\ref{tab:pretrend-ddd}), does not reject either ($F(2, 39) = 0.041$, $p = 0.960$). That test
is the one that matters for the headline estimate, and it is also the weakest of the three: with
forty occupation clusters its pre-period standard errors are of the same order as the effect being
tested, so it cannot exclude a pre-trend the size of the estimate. It is evidence of no visible
violation, not proof of none.

That is worth stating carefully, because it is true only after a correction. As described in
Section~\ref{sec:data}, the 2017 file records zero usual hours for respondents who were employed
but did not work in the reference week, a convention no later year follows. Since absence from work
is more common among mothers, those zeros depressed mothers' measured 2017 hours specifically, and
an earlier version of this analysis read the result as a significant pre-trend violation confined
to 2017 --- large enough that the identifying assumption had to be narrowed to 2018--2019. Defining
hours on reference-week workers in every year removes the artifact and the apparent violation with
it. We report this rather than silently presenting a passing test: the sensitivity of a
parallel-trends diagnostic to a single year's coding convention is itself worth knowing, and a
reader comparing these figures against an earlier draft is entitled to know why they differ.

The correction is not costless. The estimand is now usual hours among women who were working, not
among all women holding a job, and roughly $10\%$ of each year's employed sample is excluded on
that basis. If absence from work in the reference week is itself related to WFH exposure --- for
instance if teleworkable jobs make it easier to work while unwell or while caring for a child ---
then the restriction is not innocuous for the triple interaction. We find the absentee share only
mildly related to exposure across quartiles, but we cannot rule this out.

\paragraph{Lee bounds: direction and monotonicity.}
The trimming construction only handles \emph{excess} selection in the post-period mother cell ---
the direction the project's own hypothesis predicts. Under-selection in that cell (fewer employed
mothers than the counterfactual implies) is a missing-data problem, not an excess-observed-data
problem, and is not addressed. The construction assumes monotone selection (that WFH weakly
increases, never decreases, a mother's employment probability) to justify trimming from a single
tail, and computes selection rates unweighted, consistent with the rest of the analysis. A finding
of small excess selection should be read as ``this construction found little to correct,'' not as
proof that the selection concern is absent.

\paragraph{Survey weights.}
CBS survey weights are intentionally not applied in any outcome regression. This is a scope
decision, not an oversight: reported rates and coefficients are unweighted estimates on the analysis
sample, not population-representative statistics. The one exception is internal to constructing
the cell-based exposure index, where occupation exposure is aggregated to demographic cells using
the survey weight; that is not a representativeness correction for the outcome regressions.

\paragraph{The exposure anchor: 2021, not 2020.}
The realized WFH index is anchored to 2021 rather than to the 2020 variable the original research
plan specified, because no 2020 extract exists. The justification is that 2021 already passes the
validated data pipeline and is arguably a more stable measurement point than 2020's emergency
disruption. The downside is real: by 2021 occupations had had a year to adapt, and if adaptation
was uneven a 2021-based index could rank occupations' exposure differently than a 2020-based one
would have. The realized index enters only as a robustness variant; the primary calibrated index
draws on 2022--23 realized data for its swap test and is itself a documented compromise rather than
a fully pre-treatment measure.

\paragraph{Age controls and age imbalance.}
Age enters as a categorical age-group code, because the extract has no continuous age or
birth-year variable. Mothers and non-mothers are imbalanced on this code in the pre-period, and the
imbalance tracks exposure quartile; the age-interacted and reweighted specifications address this
and the estimate survives, but the reweighted attenuation ($3.224 \to 2.597$) is the largest
single move any robustness check produces, and it leaves the estimate only narrowly above the
design's minimum detectable effect of $2.864$. The result should be read as robust to age
imbalance in direction and approximate magnitude, not as insensitive to it.

\paragraph{Inference.}
The hours DDD clusters on about 40 occupation groups and the employment DDD on about 210
demographic cells; no small-cluster correction is applied. Clustering on the individual matters a
good deal more than it may appear: the survey is a rotating panel, and the hours estimation sample
of $258{,}175$ observations comes from only $64{,}602$ distinct respondents --- about four
observations each, and two to three within a single survey year, with $96\%$ of rows belonging to
someone who appears more than once. Treating those observations as independent would understate
standard errors by a factor of roughly $1.8$, which is why every interval in this paper, including
the descriptive ones in Section~\ref{sec:descriptives}, is clustered. What the panel structure does
\emph{not} support is within-person identification: an individual-level fixed-effects design was
considered and rejected because only 1.9\% of respondents are re-interviewed across the pre/post
boundary, so almost none of the repetition spans the treatment.
% Provenance: 1,514 of 80,560 distinct individuals (1.88%) appear in both the pre- and post-period.
% Console output of check_idpuf_panel_structure() (scripts/validation.R); not exported to outputs/.
% Confirmed against a full pipeline run, 2026-09-19.
Occupation codes masked by the CBS for
disclosure control are dropped when building exposure; a proxy check finds no strong evidence that
this biases the exposure measure, though that is weak rather than conclusive evidence. The
calibrated exposure measure uses post-period (2022--23) realized data, as noted.

not a uniform post-2021 shift; it is absent outside teleworkable jobs. Link to \citet{goldin2014}
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
