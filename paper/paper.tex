\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The post-pandemic shift to remote work is a persistent shock to the flexibility of employment,
the job attribute mothers have historically paid most for. We ask whether the rise of work from
home (WFH) narrowed the motherhood penalty in usual weekly hours among employed women in Israel.
We use Central Bureau of Statistics (CBS) Labor Force Survey microdata on women aged 25--59 in
2017--2019 and 2021--2023 to estimate difference-in-differences (DiD) and triple-differences (DDD)
models of usual weekly hours, with an Israeli-calibrated occupational WFH-exposure index as the
third difference. Employed mothers' hours did not change on average ($0.23$ hours), but they rose
where the work can be done from home: the triple interaction is $3.22$ hours per unit of exposure,
or $1.71$ hours a week between the mean exposures of the most and least teleworkable quartiles of
occupations. Because exposure varies across only forty occupations, we report analytic,
permutation and wild cluster bootstrap inference, and the gradient survives all three at the
$10\%$ level or better. The one exposure measure built entirely from pre-treatment information
gives a weaker, insignificant estimate: the result requires the calibration's reclassification
of teaching, which Israeli schools kept in person. The gradient is largest for mothers of
children under five, and fathers show the opposite gradient. The employment margin is estimated too imprecisely to support a comparable
conclusion. The evidence indicates that remote work raises intensive-margin labor supply for
mothers in teleworkable occupations specifically, rather than reducing the motherhood penalty
across the board.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================

The shift to remote work is a rare natural experiment in workplace flexibility. Unlike the acute
disruption of 2020, work from home has persisted after the pandemic \citep{barrero2021}, and
flexibility is the last major driver of the remaining gender pay gap \citep{goldin2014}. Mothers
pay for temporal and locational flexibility in lower wages or slower promotion. A shock that
lowers its cost should therefore be detectable in mothers' labor supply. For mothers already
employed, it should show most directly in how many hours they work. This paper asks whether the post-COVID shift to remote
work altered the motherhood penalty in \emph{usual weekly work hours} among employed women in
Israel, using Central Bureau of Statistics (CBS) Labor Force Survey microdata for 2017--2023.

One study has asked the question before, for the United States, where work from home reduced the
motherhood penalty on employment and on income \citep{harrington2025}. We ask it of a different
country and a different margin: how many hours the mothers who hold jobs supply. In doing so we
provide, to our knowledge, the first estimate of the Israeli motherhood penalty in hours
conditional on working. The occupational WFH-exposure index the design rests on is calibrated to
Israeli practice rather than imported unchanged. Israel is a natural setting for the intensive-margin question. It
has the OECD's highest total fertility rate, $2.87$ children per woman in 2024 against an OECD
average of $1.40$ \citep{oecd2025fertility}.

To identify the effect, we compare mothers and childless women across a pre-period of 2017--2019
and a post-period of 2021--2023, excluding 2020 (Section~\ref{sec:data}). A
difference-in-differences on this contrast estimates the change in the hours penalty. A
triple-differences model interacts this contrast with an occupation's WFH exposure. It tests
whether remote work is the \emph{mechanism} by asking whether the change is larger in jobs that
can actually be done from home. Its identifying assumption concerns how the motherhood gap \emph{within}
occupations would have evolved across exposure levels absent the shift.

Three findings emerge from this design. The first is a null: averaged over all employed women, the
motherhood penalty in hours is no smaller after the pandemic than before it. The second is that
the average conceals a gradient. In occupations whose work can be done from home, the hours gap
between mothers and childless women closed by roughly three fifths of the penalty in those same
occupations. In occupations tied to a workplace it did not close at all and, if anything,
widened slightly. The third is that the gradient behaves as a flexibility mechanism should. It is
largest for mothers of children under five, for whom the child-care time constraint binds most
tightly, and it declines as the youngest child ages. Fathers in the same occupations show a
gradient of the opposite sign. This rules out a shock common to everyone in teleworkable jobs, and
it is consistent with a within-household reallocation of paid and care time, though the design does
not identify one. Because the exposure score varies across only forty occupations, we report analytic,
permutation and wild cluster bootstrap inference side by side. The strength of the statistical
conclusion depends on which is used (Section~\ref{sec:res-ddd}). The estimate survives a battery of
specification, selection and leave-one-occupation-out checks and a test of sorting across
occupations. It does depend on the calibration of the exposure index in one identifiable way. US task
content scores teaching as teleworkable, but Israeli schools kept it in person. Reclassifying
teaching is what separates our estimate from the weaker, insignificant one given by the only fully
pre-treatment measure (Section~\ref{sec:res-ddd-diag}). We also estimate the employment margin, but for reasons of
statistical power documented in Section~\ref{sec:res-extensive} we treat it as secondary.

Section~\ref{sec:lit} reviews the literature, Sections~\ref{sec:data} and~\ref{sec:descriptives}
describe the data and the raw patterns, Section~\ref{sec:strategy} sets out the design,
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, and Sections~\ref{sec:discussion}--\ref{sec:conclusion}
interpret them and state their limits.

\section{Literature Review}\label{sec:lit}
%======================================================================

Three questions organize the literature this paper draws on: why the motherhood penalty
persists, what remote work does to the hours mothers supply, and how exposure to remote work can
be measured, and its effect identified, when no one is assigned to it at random.

\paragraph{The penalty and the flexibility mechanism.}
After a first birth, women's earnings fall sharply and persistently relative to men's, through
reduced hours, exit from employment and moves into family-friendly, lower-paying jobs. The size
of the penalty tracks gender norms rather than family policy
\citep{kleven2019denmark, kleven2019countries}. Because hours are one of those channels, a lower
time cost of combining work and care can move the intensive margin without moving employment.
The remaining gap has been located in the structure of jobs \citep{goldin2014}. Many occupations
pay disproportionately for long, contiguous, on-site hours, so mothers who cannot supply them pay
a penalty out of proportion to the hours they forgo. Remote work lowers the cost of supplying a
given number of hours, by removing the commute, letting hours be split around care and
permitting a virtual presence in demanding professions. Both links have empirical support.
Workers, and women in particular, give up wages for the option to work from home
\citep{maspallais2017}. The skilled gender gap is concentrated where long hours are rewarded and
narrows where substitutes for household time are cheap \citep{cortespan2019}. Lower visibility
and slower advancement could cut the other way, so the sign is an empirical question. The place
to look for it is the gradient across occupations by teleworkability, not the average.

\paragraph{What remote work does to hours, and when.}
Remote work has accounted for roughly a quarter of US paid workdays since 2021
\citep{barrero2021, barrero2023}, and the time it frees is split between paid work, care and
leisure \citep{aksoy2023}. Direct evidence on hours points both ways. Time worked rose under
randomized home working and in the pandemic move home, in the latter case without output keeping
pace \citep{bloom2015, gibbs2023}. On remote days, however, part of the saved time goes to family
and, for women, to household production, and teleworking mothers face more interruptions
\citep{pabilonia2022}. The effect on gender gaps depends on how flexibility arrived. Under
randomized or voluntary flexibility, household tasks shift toward men \citep{angelici2024}. Under
the acute 2020 shock, with schools closed, women in WFH-feasible jobs absorbed the larger share
of the resulting childcare, as was anticipated at the time \citep{alon2020, adamsprassl2020}.
Evidence on the post-acute period, once schools reopened and remote work became a job attribute
rather than a lockdown condition, is much thinner. The one study of the motherhood penalty in
that period \citep{harrington2025} finds that WFH raised US mothers' employment and income, with
the employment effect concentrated in fields with high returns to hours and inflexible time
demands. Hours are one channel of that income result, and the margin this paper observes
directly.

\paragraph{Measuring exposure and identifying its effect.}
Remote work is chosen, and who selects into it differs sharply from what it does to a given
worker \citep{emanuelharrington2024}. A credible design therefore measures \emph{exposure} from
the content of jobs rather than from realized remote-work status. Two measures are in use. The
ex ante measure asks whether an occupation's tasks can all be done at home, a criterion over a
third of US employment meets \citep{dingel2020}. The ex post measure is the share of an
occupation's workers who actually work from home. The first is immune to selection but identical
across countries; the second reflects local practice but is itself a labor-market outcome. They
diverge where institutions overrode task content. Teaching is the case in point: task content
scores it as fully teleworkable, but Israeli schools kept it in person. Our index starts from the
task-based classification mapped to two-digit ISCO-08 codes and calibrates it against realized
Israeli remote work (Section~\ref{sec:exposure}), for which a pre-pandemic baseline exists
\citep{madhala2020}. A continuous exposure score also places the design in the
continuous-treatment difference-in-differences family, where a single slope compares outcomes
across exposure levels and need not weight those comparisons positively \citep{callaway2024}.
Section~\ref{sec:strategy} takes up the two results that bear on reading our coefficient.

\paragraph{Israel, and the gap this paper fills.}
The Israeli evidence concerns other margins and other shocks. The gender wage gap is driven
mainly by women's sorting into lower-paying firms, even within industry, with pay differences
within firms contributing little \citep{buzaglo2023}. An occupation-level exposure measure
therefore captures only part of the relevant heterogeneity. Motherhood penalties in employment
and earnings differ across ethno-religious groups \citep{budig2023}, and the 2020 lockdown
reallocated paid and unpaid work within households in gender-unequal ways \citep{yaish2021}. To
our knowledge, there is no published Israeli estimate of the motherhood penalty in hours
conditional on working. Nor has any Israeli study asked whether remote work changed any margin
of the penalty. This paper supplies both, with post-acute evidence from a high-fertility economy.
It also shows, through the teaching occupations, that calibrating the exposure index to local
practice can decide the result (Section~\ref{sec:res-ddd-diag}).

\section{Data and Institutional Context}\label{sec:data}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Source and sample}

The data are individual-level microdata from the Israeli Central Bureau of Statistics Labor Force
Survey for the survey years 2017--2019 and 2021--2023. The analysis sample is women aged 25--59
(CBS age-group codes 3--7). A woman is a \emph{mother} if she has any child aged 17 or younger in the
household and childless otherwise; \emph{Post} equals one for survey years 2021 and later. The
comparison group therefore includes women whose children are grown and women who become mothers
within the window (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The year 2020 is excluded because its
arrangements were emergency improvisation, not the settled practice we want to measure, and
because no 2020 CBS extract exists for this project.

\noindent The primary outcome is \emph{usual weekly work hours}, recorded by the CBS in bins and assigned
each bin's median (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-data} gives the coding and a 2017 recording correction).
Hours are the only intensive-margin outcome the survey supports: the extract records how a wage
is paid but not its amount. Hours are defined for women who are employed \emph{and} worked in the
survey's reference week, in every year, a harmonization made necessary by a different zero-hours
recording convention in the 2017 extract (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-data}). It costs about $10\%$ of
each year's employed sample and narrows the estimand to usual hours among women who were working
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The secondary outcome is a binary employment indicator
(unemployed and not-in-labor-force are both zero).

All regressions include the same categorical controls: marital status, religion, age group,
district of residence and highest educational attainment (six groups); age enters as the CBS
age-group code because the extract contains no continuous age. CBS survey weights are
deliberately not applied in any outcome regression (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The
replication code is available in the project's Git
repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/NitzanZacharia/labor-econ-remote-work-analysis-hours.git}}

\subsection{Constructing WFH exposure}\label{sec:exposure}

Realized WFH status is itself a labor-market outcome, jointly determined with hours, so the
mechanism test needs an occupation-level measure of \emph{exposure} to remote work that the
individual does not choose. The rule that selects the primary measure is fixed ex ante: the
headline score must be anchored on pre-period task content, so that it cannot be built from the
post-2021 hours it is meant to explain, and must not place in the treated tail occupations that
Israeli institutions kept in person. We build three measures and report each.

\noindent \emph{External.} The Dingel--Neiman teleworkability score \citep{dingel2020} at the
two-digit ISCO-08 level. This is pre-period by construction, but it is a US task-based measure
and misclassifies occupations where Israeli practice diverged, so it fails the second condition:
teaching scores $0.966$ on it, yet Israeli schools were fully in person throughout the 2022--23
survey years (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-exposure}).

\emph{Realized.} The realized Israeli WFH share by occupation, anchored to 2021. Post-treatment
by construction, it fails the first condition.

\emph{Calibrated (primary).} This index replaces the external score with the realized 2022--23
Israeli WFH share only where the two are far apart and the gap is statistically distinguishable
from noise; ten of the forty occupations are swapped, teaching and clerical support among them
(rule, scores and fit in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-exposure}). It is the only measure that meets
both conditions, so it is primary, and the other two are reported as bounds in
Section~\ref{sec:res-ddd-diag}. Because the swap test draws on 2022--23 data, inside the
post-period, the calibrated measure is \emph{partly post-treatment}; Section~\ref{sec:res-ddd-diag}
tests whether the calibration is what produces the result.

A fourth measure serves three roles. For the employment DDD, occupation is undefined for the
non-employed, so that specification uses a pre-period (2017--2019) shift-share index by
demographic cell (sex, age group, education, district, marital status, religion and continent of
birth; 8,884 cells), equal to the mean of the calibrated occupation score over the cell's
pre-period occupational composition and defined for employed and non-employed women alike. The
same index stratifies the selection correction of Section~\ref{sec:lee}, and because a woman's
post-2021 occupation cannot move it, it also bounds occupational sorting in
Section~\ref{sec:res-ddd-diag}. Its dispersion is a third of the occupation-level index's, so
coefficients on the two are compared per standard deviation, not per unit.

\subsection{The raw gap and its exposure gradient}\label{sec:desc-hours}

Mothers work $38.54$ hours per week before 2021 and $38.84$ after, a gain of $0.31$ hours;
childless women move from $40.04$ to $40.08$. The difference between those two changes,
$0.27$ hours, is the difference-in-differences of equation~\eqref{eq:did} without a single
control, and the regression DiD of Table~\ref{tab:hours}, $0.228$ (SE $0.181$), is within four
hundredths of an hour of it. Mothers remain well below childless women in both periods, and the
raw gap widened by $0.3$ hours over the three pre-period years, within sampling error
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:hours-by-year}).

\noindent Figure~\ref{fig:dose-response} computes the same difference-in-differences separately within
each quartile of occupational WFH exposure. The bottom three quartiles return $-0.32$, $0.27$ and
$-0.02$ hours, none distinguishable from zero; the top quartile returns $1.55$ hours (95\% CI
$[0.74, 2.36]$). The near-zero average is therefore a weighted average of three nulls and one
substantial positive difference. The figure is a description rather than an independent causal
test: occupational composition and time-varying confounders can differ across quartiles, and
the regressions of Section~\ref{sec:results} address that. The response is not linear in
exposure: the bottom three quartiles span exposure values of $0.00$ to $0.30$ and the top
quartile $0.36$ to $0.75$, so most of the regressor's dispersion, and the identifying contrast,
sits in one tail.

\section{Empirical Strategy}\label{sec:strategy}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Difference-in-differences}

For outcome $Y_{it}$ (weekly hours conditional on working that week, or employment) we estimate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:did}
Y_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \Mother_i + \beta_2 \Post_t + \beta_3 (\Mother_i \times \Post_t)
+ X'_{it}\gamma + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
where $X_{it}$ is the control vector of Section~\ref{sec:data} and $\beta_3$ is the differential
change in the outcome for mothers relative to childless women after the shift to remote work.

\subsection{Triple differences}

The flexibility framework \citep{goldin2014} yields one testable hypothesis: after 2021,
mothers' usual hours conditional on working rise relative to childless women's in proportion to
their occupation's WFH exposure, with no response where the work cannot be done from home. The
exposure gradient, not the average change, is the object of interest, so we interact the DiD with
occupational WFH exposure:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ddd}
\begin{split}
Y_{it} = {}& \alpha + \delta_1 \Mother_i + \delta_2 \Post_t + \delta_3 \WFH_{j(i)}
+ \delta_4 (\Mother_i \times \Post_t) \\
& + \delta_5 (\Mother_i \times \WFH_{j(i)}) + \delta_6 (\Post_t \times \WFH_{j(i)}) \\
& + \delta_7 (\Mother_i \times \Post_t \times \WFH_{j(i)}) + X'_{it}\gamma + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $j(i)$ is individual $i$'s occupation. $\delta_7$ asks whether the post-shift change for
mothers relative to non-mothers is larger in more exposed occupations; $\delta_6$ absorbs any
post-2021 change in teleworkable occupations common to mothers and childless women, $\delta_5$
the pre-existing level of the penalty across occupations, and $\delta_4$ is the post-shift change
for mothers at \emph{zero} exposure, not an average effect. The DDD requires a single
parallel-trends assumption \citep{olden2022}: that the mother/non-mother gap in high- versus
low-exposure jobs would have evolved in parallel absent the shift to remote work. Insignificant
pre-period coefficients are evidence about that assumption, not a verification of it. For the
hours DDD, $\WFH_j$ is the calibrated occupation-level score and the sample is employed women
matched to a two-digit occupation; for the employment DDD, $\WFH$ is the cell-based index, the
sample is all women, and a $\Mother \times$ age-group interaction is added for the pre-period age
imbalance.

\noindent Equation~\eqref{eq:ddd} makes each lower-order occupation-level difference linear in exposure,
an unnecessary restriction with forty occupations. A single slope also compares effects
\emph{across} exposure levels, which requires the stronger condition that occupations at
different exposure levels would have followed the same path under any common level of exposure,
and it is a weighted average of such comparisons whose weights need not be positive
\citep{callaway2024}. We therefore estimate two relaxations alongside it. The
\emph{saturated} specification replaces every lower-order term with fixed effects,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ddd-sat}
Y_{it} = \delta_7 (\Mother_i \times \Post_t \times \WFH_{j(i)}) + X'_{it}\gamma
+ \lambda_{j(i),t} + \mu_{j(i),\Mother_i} + \nu_{\Mother_i,t} + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{jt}$, $\mu_{j,m}$ and $\nu_{m,t}$ are occupation-by-year, occupation-by-mother
and mother-by-year effects, which absorb every lower-order term of equation~\eqref{eq:ddd} and
any occupation-specific year shock. The \emph{binned} specification replaces $\WFH_{j(i)}$ with
indicators for the pre-period exposure quartile the occupation falls in, the bins of
Figure~\ref{fig:dose-response}, so that the top-versus-bottom contrast carries a standard error.
For each DDD we also report the minimum detectable effect on the reference distribution the
model's inference uses, $\mathrm{MDE} = \mathrm{SE}\,(t_{0.975} + t_{0.80})$, which is
$2.87 \times \mathrm{SE}$ with 39 degrees of freedom and $2.80 \times \mathrm{SE}$ under the
normal, so that a null can be read as ``genuinely null'' or ``underpowered.''

\subsection{Inference}\label{sec:inference}

The survey is a rotating panel in which most respondents appear about four times, so
equation~\eqref{eq:did} and every descriptive interval are clustered by individual
(Appendix~\ref{sec:app-data} gives the panel counts). For the hours DDD the regressor is constant
within a two-digit occupation, and standard errors are clustered at that level, leaving forty
clusters; the reported $p$-values use a $t$ reference distribution with $G - 1 = 39$ degrees of
freedom and a cluster-count adjustment to the variance. Forty is few enough that we do not rest on
that approximation: for the headline coefficient and for the pre-period coefficients of the
triple-difference event study we also report wild cluster bootstrap $p$-values, with Rademacher
weights, the null imposed and $9{,}999$ draws \citep{roodman2019, fischer2021}, and
Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics} adds a permutation test that relies on no asymptotic approximation
at all. The same bootstrap is applied to every occupation-clustered robustness and subgroup
refit and printed beside its analytic $p$-value, since a star from a test that over-rejects is
weaker evidence than it looks. The three procedures answer related but different questions, and
where they disagree the disagreement is part of the evidence. The joint pre-period test remains the analytic Wald $F$,
since a joint bootstrap test is not available in the implementation used. The employment DDD is
clustered on the age-by-education-by-district cell (about 210 clusters).

\subsection{Selection into employment: Lee bounds}\label{sec:lee}

Hours are observed only for women who are working, and working is itself an outcome of the
same treatment. We therefore report trimming bounds \citep{lee2009}: the share
of post-period mothers observed with hours in excess of a parallel-trends counterfactual is
trimmed from the top of that cell's hours distribution for a lower bound and from the bottom for
an upper bound, the model is refit, and the Imbens--Manski interval for the identified set
\citep{imbens2004} is reported. For the DDD the trimming is done within quartiles of the
cell-based index, which is defined for the non-employed. Appendix~\ref{sec:app-lee} gives the
construction and its
assumptions. The bounds address excess selection under monotonicity; they cannot address
selection that violates it, nor differences in absence unrelated to excess selection.

\subsection{Diagnostics, falsification, and the fathers' comparison}\label{sec:diagnostics}

We estimate event-study versions of equation~\eqref{eq:did} with $\Mother \times$ year
interactions (reference year 2019) for both outcomes and test the pre-period coefficients
jointly; because those test the difference-in-differences assumption rather than the
triple-differences one, we also estimate the event-study version of equation~\eqref{eq:ddd}
itself, with $\Post$ replaced by survey-year interactions at every level, and apply the same
joint test to its pre-period coefficients.

\noindent The falsification test is a permutation test on the exposure score. Under the sharp null that
teleworkability is unrelated to the post-2021 change in the motherhood gap, the forty occupation
scores are exchangeable, so we reassign them at random across the forty occupation codes $999$
times, holding every woman's occupation and the mother/period structure fixed, refit
equation~\eqref{eq:ddd}, and record the cluster-robust $t$-statistic of the triple interaction;
the permutation $p$-value is the share of draws, counting the observed one, whose $|t|$ reaches
the observed value \citep{phipson2010}. Because occupation is observed when the outcome is, we
also check whether remote work drew mothers into teleworkable occupations after 2021, with a
difference-in-differences that takes the exposure score itself as the outcome, and bound the
channel by re-estimating the hours DDD on the pre-period cell index of
Section~\ref{sec:exposure}.

\noindent The remaining checks, collected in Table~\ref{tab:robust}, re-estimate the hours DDD with the
exposure measure, the age control, the sample, the outcome coding and the inference procedure
varied in turn, and re-estimate it by the age of the youngest child, separately for Jewish and
Arab women, and on the male subsample with ``mother'' read as ``father.'' The last is a
comparison, not a placebo: fathers in teleworkable occupations are exposed to remote work exactly
as mothers are, so a negative gradient would be evidence on household reallocation rather than on
a confound. Two-sample $z$-tests compare coefficients across disjoint subsamples.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
%======================================================================

Three findings organize this section. The average motherhood penalty in weekly hours did not
change after the shift to remote work. It did narrow where the work can be done from home: the
post-2021 change in the mother/childless gap rises with an occupation's WFH exposure, with the
response concentrated in the most teleworkable quartile of occupations. That gradient survives the
design's checks on selection, sorting, calibration and specification, is largest for mothers of
young children and reversed in sign for fathers, while the employment margin is too imprecisely
estimated to support a comparable conclusion. Tables~\ref{tab:hours}--\ref{tab:subgroup} and
Figure~\ref{fig:pretrend} carry the estimates; the text reports the pattern. Significance is
denoted \sym{***} $p<0.001$, \sym{**} $p<0.01$, \sym{*} $p<0.05$, \sym{\cdot} $p<0.1$, with
standard errors in parentheses. Stars follow the analytic occupation-clustered $t$-test; because
that test over-rejects with forty clusters (Section~\ref{sec:inference}),
Tables~\ref{tab:robust}--\ref{tab:subgroup} also print a wild cluster bootstrap $p$-value beside
every occupation-clustered triple interaction.

\subsection{No average change in the hours penalty}\label{sec:res-did}

Column~(1) of Table~\ref{tab:hours} estimates equation~\eqref{eq:did}. Mothers work $1.739$ fewer
hours per week than childless women, and that penalty did not measurably change after 2021:
$\Mother \times \Post$ is $0.228$ (SE $0.181$); its 95\% confidence interval, $[-0.13, 0.58]$,
excludes average gains above about half an hour a week, and the design could have detected an
effect of about $0.51$ hours. Panel~(a) of Figure~\ref{fig:pretrend} supports the comparison: the
pre-period coefficients are indistinguishable from zero and pass a joint Wald test
($F(2, 63{,}198) = 0.949$, $p = 0.387$), and the post-period path is flat apart from a larger 2023
coefficient. Survey-year effects in place of the single $\Post$ dummy, and the selection bounds of
Appendix~\ref{sec:app-lee}, leave the reading unchanged. Nothing that follows rests on this
average.

\subsection{Hours rose where the work can be done from home}\label{sec:res-ddd}

Column~(2) of Table~\ref{tab:hours} interacts the DiD with the calibrated occupation-level exposure
index. The triple interaction is $3.224$\sym{**} (SE $1.022$): among working mothers, those in more
WFH-exposed occupations saw a larger post-2021 increase in weekly hours relative to childless women
in the same occupations. That is $0.64$ hours per standard deviation of the index ($0.199$), and
$1.71$ hours between the mean exposures of the bottom and top quartiles of
Figure~\ref{fig:dose-response}, the pattern that figure shows in raw means. The implied
change at zero exposure is $-0.539$ (SE $0.336$), so mothers in jobs that cannot be done from home
gained nothing, and the gradient is net of whatever happened to hours in teleworkable occupations
for all women. What changed after 2021 is not mothers' hours in general but mothers' hours where
remote work was possible.

Columns~(3) and~(4) relax the functional form and tell the same story. With the three sets of
fixed effects of equation~\eqref{eq:ddd-sat} absorbing every lower-order term, the triple
interaction is $2.876$ (SE $0.950$), a tenth below the pooled estimate and still significant at
the $1\%$ level. With quartile indicators in place of the
score, the post-2021 change in the gap relative to the least teleworkable quartile is positive but
insignificant in the second and third quartiles and $1.844$ hours (SE $0.595$, $p < 0.01$) in the
top one: a step at the top of the exposure distribution more than a smooth gradient. That quartile
holds six of the forty occupations, but the pooled and saturated columns, which use all forty,
agree with it, and no single occupation drives it (Section~\ref{sec:res-robust}).

Panel~(b) of Figure~\ref{fig:pretrend} is the event-study version of equation~\eqref{eq:ddd}, the
pre-trend test the triple difference itself requires (Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}). The
pre-period coefficients are near zero and jointly insignificant ($F(2, 39) = 0.041$, $p = 0.960$;
wild cluster bootstrap $p = 0.85$ on their sum), and the post-period path steps up in 2021 and
rises through 2023, every year straddling the pooled estimate. The test has limited power: with forty clusters the pre-period standard errors are of the
same order as the effect, so each pre-period interval contains values as large as the estimate
itself. It shows no sign of a violation; it does not rule one out.

How precisely the gradient is estimated depends on the inference procedure, and we report three.
The analytic standard error, clustered by occupation on a $t_{39}$ reference, puts the estimate
$3.15$ standard errors from zero ($p = 0.003$). The two procedures built for forty clusters are
less emphatic. The permutation test gives $p = 0.032$: $31$ of $999$ random reassignments of the
forty scores across occupations produce a $|t|$ at least as large as the observed one (Appendix
Figure~\ref{fig:permutation}). The wild cluster
bootstrap gives $p = 0.056$, with a 95\% confidence set of $[-0.26, 5.84]$. We report all three
rather than choosing among them.

\subsection{Selection, sorting, and the calibrated regressor}\label{sec:res-ddd-diag}

Three features of the design could produce an exposure gradient with no change in mothers' labor
supply: selection into work, sorting of mothers across occupations, and an index calibrated on
post-period data. Table~\ref{tab:robust} reports the tests.

\paragraph{Selection into work.}
Trimming the excess
selection among post-period mothers within each quartile of the cell-based index
(Appendix~\ref{sec:app-lee}) barely moves the estimate in either direction, and the Imbens--Manski
95\% interval for the identified set, $[1.021, 5.324]$, excludes zero.

\paragraph{Sorting across occupations.}
If remote work drew mothers into teleworkable occupations after 2021, the occupation score would
itself respond, and it does not: as a dependent variable it gives a $\Mother \times \Post$ under
one hundredth of its standard deviation, the probability of being in the top quartile moves by
$0.4$ percentage points (SE $0.7$), and the event-study version is flat in every year. The hours
DDD on the pre-period demographic-cell index, which a woman's later occupation cannot move, is
positive but imprecise ($0.139$ hours per standard deviation of that index, SE $0.159$); that index
is a far coarser measure of a woman's own teleworkability, so the sorting DiD is the informative
test.

\paragraph{Calibration.}
The calibrated index draws on realized 2022--23 WFH shares, so the regressor could be absorbing
post-period outcomes. The external Dingel--Neiman index \citep{dingel2020}, the only measure
built entirely from pre-treatment information, gives $0.672$ (SE $0.884$), positive but
insignificant and the weakest
of the three exposure measures; the realized 2021-anchored index gives a larger, noisier estimate.
Three checks hold the sample at all forty occupations. Calibrating on men only, so that no woman in
the regression contributes to her own regressor, gives $3.869$\sym{***} (SE $0.979$). Keeping the
external score but letting the ten occupations the calibration reclassifies carry their own
$\Mother \times \Post$ change restores a gradient in the external score at nearly three quarters
of the headline ($p = 0.09$): the occupations the external index places in the treated tail did not
respond. Reclassifying teaching alone, with the other thirty-nine occupations at their external
scores, gives $1.934$\sym{***} (SE $0.519$). Dropping the ten reclassified occupations instead,
so that the two indices coincide on the thirty that remain, gives $4.309$\sym{**} (SE $1.192$),
and moving the calibration's swap threshold from $0.30$ to $0.90$ leaves the estimate positive
throughout, from $2.3$ with only teaching and clerical support swapped to $3.9$ (SE $2.8$) with
fifteen occupations swapped (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:calibration-sweep}). The result therefore
requires reclassifying teaching, and that reclassification rests on an institutional fact
rather than on any woman's hours: Israeli schools were fully in person in 2022--23
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-exposure}). The calibration cannot be made into a pre-treatment
measure, and a reader who weights the external index above the others will read the headline as
directionally supported rather than established.

\subsection{Robustness of the gradient}\label{sec:res-robust}

The remaining checks in Table~\ref{tab:robust} vary the age and marital controls, the sample and
the outcome coding, and two of them matter. Mothers and childless women differ in age, education
and marital status in ways that themselves vary with exposure (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:balance-quartile}), and such a gap is what the triple interaction is identified
off. A $\Mother \times$ age-group interaction leaves the estimate essentially unchanged, but
reweighting to balance pre-period age groups attenuates it by about 19\% to $2.597$\sym{*}
(SE $1.050$; bootstrap $p = 0.05$), significant at the 5\% level on the analytic test but
below the design's minimum detectable effect.

Marital status is the imbalance the age checks do not touch: $85\%$ of mothers are married
against $47\%$ of childless women (Table~\ref{tab:descriptives}), and the gap widens with
exposure. Restricting the sample to married women gives $2.743$ (SE $1.512$, $p = 0.08$), and
adding marital status $\times \Post \times \WFH$ to the specification gives $2.191$
(SE $1.273$, $p = 0.09$): the gradient stays positive but loses conventional significance. Part
of it, then, is plausibly the comparison group rather than mothers, if never-married childless
women in teleworkable jobs, who make up a larger share of that group at high exposure, worked
fewer hours after 2021. Neither row is a clean test. The interaction is partly over-control,
since most mothers are married and the term is estimated partly off them, and the married
subsample trades a marital confound for a life-stage one, since married childless women are
older and often have grown children. We report both as the bounded version of a weakness the
design cannot remove.

Everything else stays close to the headline. Excluding the 2023 survey year, whose final quarter
includes the start of the October 2023 war (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), gives $2.979$\sym{*}
(SE $1.170$), while the plain hours DiD stays a null. Removing each of the forty occupations in
turn (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:loo}) leaves the estimate between $2.415$ and $3.580$, within one
standard error of the headline and significant at the 5\% level on the analytic test in every
refit ($p \leq 0.017$).
Across every specification in the table that keeps a calibrated index, the triple interaction lies
between $2.2$ and $4.3$ hours. The wild cluster bootstrap column of Table~\ref{tab:robust}
tempers the stars: the calibrated-index rows range from $p = 0.02$ (unswapped occupations, men-only calibration) to
$p = 0.13$ (marital interaction), with the remaining sample and outcome-coding rows all within
two hundredths of the headline's own $0.056$.

Nor does the outcome coding drive it. Hours are a bin-median approximation and the effect is
smaller than one bin width, but dropping the two imputed irregular-hours codes changes little, and
because 35 and 40 hours are exact bin edges the same specification can be estimated as a linear
probability model on either threshold: the probability of usually working at least 35 hours, and at
least 40, rises by about $2$ percentage points per standard deviation of exposure ($1.9$ and $2.1$)
for mothers relative to childless women after 2021. The top of the distribution matters too.
The open $60$-plus bin is assigned $78.5$ hours, the centre of its range, a coding choice rather
than a measurement; recoding it to $60$ gives $2.589$\sym{**} (SE $0.917$), a fifth below the
headline, and a linear probability model for being in that bin gives a triple interaction of
$0.034$\sym{***} (SE $0.009$), so mothers' share of the longest-hours bin rose relative to
childless women's where the work can be done from home. The effect therefore appears both at
the full-time boundary and at the top of the distribution, and its size depends in part on the
value assigned to the open bin.

\subsection{Who responded: child age, population group, and fathers}\label{sec:res-childage}

If remote work relaxes a time constraint that binds through child care, the response should be
largest for mothers of young children and smallest for mothers of teenagers, and it is.
Table~\ref{tab:childage} re-estimates the DiD and DDD for mothers whose youngest child is aged
0--4, 5--9, 10--14 or 15--17, each against the same childless women. The average effect is a null
in every group, and the exposure gradient declines monotonically with the child's age, from
$3.656$\sym{**} hours per unit of exposure (SE $1.079$) for mothers of under-fives to $1.862$
(SE $0.935$) for mothers of 15--17-year-olds, half as large. The four regressions share a control
group and the same forty clusters, so their intervals overlap and are not independent, and on the
wild cluster bootstrap none of the four is significant at the 5\% level (bootstrap $p$ from
$0.06$ for the under-fives to $0.10$ for the oldest group). The pattern is a gradient consistent
with the mechanism rather than a set of statistically separated effects, and the estimate is not
carried by mothers of teenagers.

\label{sec:res-subgroup}Table~\ref{tab:subgroup} splits the sample by population group and
re-estimates the models on men. Jewish women reproduce the pooled pattern (bootstrap $p = 0.06$).
Arab women's gradient is nominally twice as large but statistically indistinguishable from it
($z = 0.942$, $p = 0.35$) on a subsample nine times smaller, and its bootstrap $p$ is $0.14$:
imprecise rather than a documented heterogeneous effect.

For men, with ``mother'' read as ``father,'' the gradient has the opposite sign, $-1.762$
(SE $1.015$, analytic $p = 0.09$, bootstrap $p = 0.15$), and is distinguishable from the
mothers' ($z = 3.461$, $p = 0.0005$). Fathers' average hours also fell relative to childless
men's, a DiD of $-0.400$\sym{*} (SE $0.188$) where mothers' average did not move, and the
women-versus-men difference in the DiD is itself significant ($z = 2.406$, $p = 0.016$).
The comparison is not a placebo (Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}), so a null was never the
prediction. What it shows is that a macro trend common to all workers in teleworkable jobs cannot
produce a positive gradient for mothers beside a negative one for fathers, and that opposite
gradients are what within-household reallocation would look like if mothers' additional paid hours
were matched by fathers' additional care hours. We report that as a reading the data are consistent
with, not a result the design identifies: mothers and fathers are not linked in the data, and the
fathers' coefficient is itself imprecise.

\subsection{The extensive margin}\label{sec:res-extensive}

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:extensive} reports equation~\eqref{eq:did} with employment as the outcome.
The DiD is $-0.0053$ (SE $0.0052$), an informative null with a standard error of half a percentage
point whose pre-period coefficients pass their joint test. The employment DDD with the cell-based
index is a null of a different kind: the design could detect a triple interaction no smaller than
$1.43$ percentage points per standard deviation of the cell index, which is about $2.0$
percentage points per tenth of a unit of exposure given the index's standard deviation of
$0.07$; against the US estimate of $0.78$ percentage points per 10\% rise in WFH
\citep{harrington2025} it is underpowered by a factor of about $2.6$. The shortfall is structural, a binary outcome and a regressor with a
standard deviation of $0.07$, and re-clustering, combining the two margins and refining the cell
partition did not close it. The employment DDD is therefore \emph{uninformative}, evidence neither
for nor against a WFH-mediated employment effect, and the two nulls should not be read as one
verdict.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
%======================================================================

Among mothers who remain in work there is no post-2021 hours effect on average, and a large one
in teleworkable occupations: a triple interaction of about $3.2$ hours per unit of exposure and
an implied change at zero exposure that is if anything negative. This is consistent with WFH access relaxing a binding time constraint for
mothers in teleworkable jobs, the flexibility mechanism \citep{goldin2014}, and hard to
reconcile with a
general recovery common to all women, which would have raised hours across occupations rather
than only where the work can be done from home. The checks of the preceding section establish
that the gradient is not an artifact of one specification, one occupation, one sample or one
outcome coding. They are not independent confirmations: every one is estimated on the same forty
occupation clusters as the headline, and where a wild cluster bootstrap $p$-value is available it
lies above the 5\% level, from $0.06$ for the youngest child-age group to $0.15$ for the
fathers' gradient, with the headline itself at $0.056$
(Tables~\ref{tab:robust}--\ref{tab:subgroup}). How precisely the gradient is estimated remains
the question the three inference procedures answer differently. It is identified off a concrete
contrast: teaching and clerical support, which Israeli practice kept in person, placed low
against the professional occupations placed high. Among the latter, science and engineering
professionals (ISCO 21) carry the most weight, since removing them gives the leave-one-out
minimum of $2.415$, while ICT professionals, high on every exposure measure, work slightly
against the gradient, since removing them raises it to $3.287$ (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:loo}).

\noindent The magnitude is economically meaningful where it applies. For a mother at the top quartile's
mean exposure the regression implies a post-2021 change of about $1.4$ hours a week
($-0.54 + 3.22 \times 0.59$), and the raw top-quartile difference-in-differences of
Figure~\ref{fig:dose-response} is $1.55$. That $1.4$ is the fitted change \emph{at} top-quartile
exposure, distinct from the $1.71$-hour fitted contrast \emph{between} the quartile means. The
relevant penalty is the one in those same jobs, not the average:
column~(2) of Table~\ref{tab:hours} puts the pre-period
motherhood gap at $-1.45 - 1.30 \times 0.59 \approx -2.2$ hours at that exposure, so mothers in
the most teleworkable jobs recovered roughly three fifths of their penalty after 2021. At the
bottom quartile's mean exposure of $0.06$ the implied change is $-0.35$ hours: mothers in the
least teleworkable jobs recovered none of it. Commuting time alone does not account for the
size. Workers save about 72 minutes per day worked from home and return roughly 40\% of that
time to their jobs, the rest going to leisure and care \citep{aksoy2023}, so two remote days a
week add about one hour of paid work, not two and a half. The $1.4$ hours also averages over
\emph{all} mothers in top-quartile jobs, and even in those occupations only a minority of
workers are at home in the reference week (the pooled shares by occupation in Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:exposure-scores}), so the response per mother who actually works from home is
several times larger than the commute she saves. That points to the channel of the framework in
Section~\ref{sec:lit}: flexibility in \emph{when} hours are worked, hours split around care,
rather than travel time returned to work. The timing is
consistent with a multi-year adjustment: the DDD event study rises from $3.0$ in 2021 to $4.0$
in 2023 (on the 2023 year, see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\noindent A general recovery is not the only alternative to that mechanism; three demand-side accounts
also fit a gradient by teleworkability. Employers in professional and ICT occupations may have
changed the contracts they offer mothers after 2021, with no change in mothers' constraints; the
saturated specification absorbs any occupation-by-year shock common to mothers and childless
women, so this needs a change aimed at mothers in teleworkable occupations specifically. The
2021--22 technology hiring boom raised hours in exposed occupations for everyone; the fathers'
opposite-signed gradient rules out a shock common to all workers in those occupations, but not
one that fell on mothers alone. And usual hours are self-reported: if mothers working from home
report the hours they are available while fathers report the hours they work, the two gradients
would diverge as observed with no change in labor supplied. The child-age gradient does not
separate the two readings: teleworking mothers of young children report more interruptions to
the workday \citep{pabilonia2022}, so the gap between hours available and hours worked would
itself be largest for mothers of infants, the pattern the gradient shows. The fathers' gradient
and the saturated specification are the arguments that remain. None of the three is excluded by
the design, and we read the exposure
gradient as the time-constraint mechanism's signature rather than its proof.

\noindent Why hours and not employment? The employment DDD is not a failed test of the mechanism but an
uninformative one (Section~\ref{sec:res-extensive}). Substantively the two results are coherent:
the effect operates on \emph{how much} mothers in teleworkable jobs work, not on \emph{whether}
they are employed, which is a leading channel of the child penalty \citep{kleven2019denmark},
and it is where our findings depart from the US evidence on employment and income
\citep{harrington2025}. The policy reading is correspondingly narrow: WFH
availability matters for how intensively mothers who already hold \emph{teleworkable} jobs can
work, not for whether mothers in general hold jobs; and access to those jobs is itself unequally
distributed across mothers, so the population-wide gain is smaller than the gradient alone
suggests and cannot be sized without representative weighting.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{Identification and inference.}
The triple difference's own pre-trend test (Section~\ref{sec:res-ddd}) shows no visible
violation but lacks the power to rule one out. The exposure measure that delivers the headline
is calibrated on post-period Israeli data, and the result requires reclassifying teaching. The
fully pre-treatment external index yields a positive and insignificant estimate, and the checks
of Section~\ref{sec:res-ddd-diag} bound how much of the gradient the reclassification carries.
The sorting check finds no post-2021 movement of mothers across exposure levels, but the
pre-period cell index bounds the effect only imprecisely. The headline's precision is the range
of the three inference procedures in Section~\ref{sec:res-ddd}, not the analytic $p$-value
alone. The stars in the supporting rows of Tables~\ref{tab:robust}--\ref{tab:subgroup} come from
the analytic test, and the bootstrap $p$-values beside them mostly lie above $0.05$. The
permutation test assumes that the forty exposure scores are exchangeable across occupations
under its null.

\paragraph{Exposure measurement.}
Exposure is assigned at the two-digit occupation level, forty groups, whereas sorting across
firms, even within the same industry, is a first-order driver of Israeli gender gaps
\citep{buzaglo2023}. Assigning every woman her occupation's mean teleworkability is Berkson-type
error, which does not by itself bias the slope. Error in the occupation score itself attenuates
the triple interaction toward zero. If mothers sort into more or less teleworkable jobs
\emph{within} an occupation, the bias can run in either direction. The calibrated score also
mixes two scales (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-exposure}). Occupation codes masked by the CBS for
disclosure control are dropped when building exposure. A proxy check finds a small,
statistically detectable difference in remote work between masked and unmasked rows
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-exposure}).

\paragraph{The narrowed hours estimand.}
Hours are usual hours among women who were working, not among all women holding a job, and
roughly $10\%$ of each year's employed sample is excluded on that basis. The exclusion is not
innocuous for the triple interaction, because absence is related to exposure. Absence is highest
in the least teleworkable quartile, and among mothers it fell after 2021 in the top quartile
while rising in the bottom one (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-data}). The bounds of Section~\ref{sec:lee}
are therefore bounds on selection into the observed-hours sample. They handle only \emph{excess}
selection under monotonicity, and finding little to correct is not the same as the concern being
absent. The absence margin can be checked directly, because employed women absent from work in
the reference week still carry an occupation code. Adding them at their reported usual hours on
2018--2023, the years in which the survey recorded those hours, gives $3.076$\sym{***} (SE
$0.861$; bootstrap $p = 0.07$) against the headline's $3.224$ (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). The
reference-week restriction is therefore not what produces the gradient. Trimming bounds on the
absence margin itself are left for future work.

\paragraph{Sample, period and comparison group.}
The 2023 coefficient is the largest in both event studies, and the 2023 survey year includes the
quarter in which the war that began on 7 October 2023 disrupted the labor market. Reserve
call-ups bear on the fathers' comparison, and school closures and evacuations bear on mothers'
hours. The estimate survives dropping the year, and 2021 and 2022 are individually significant
on the triple difference. The 2023 point should nonetheless be held more loosely. Early 2021
likewise included a third lockdown, so the post-period is ``post-acute'' more cleanly in its
later years than in its first. The comparison group includes women whose children are grown and
women who become mothers within the window, as in the US study \citep{harrington2025}. If part
of it responds to the treatment as mothers do, the DiD is biased toward zero. Mothers and
non-mothers are imbalanced on the categorical age code in a way that tracks exposure (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:balance-quartile}). The age-interacted and reweighted specifications of
Section~\ref{sec:res-robust} address this. The estimate stays positive in both but, after
reweighting, falls below the design's minimum detectable effect. Marital status is imbalanced in
the same direction, and the two marital checks of that section leave the gradient positive but
only marginally significant.
% The exclusion is of the whole year: the survey-month field (ChodeshSeker) is dropped at cleaning,
% so a fourth-quarter-only exclusion would need a schema change (audit item I5).

\paragraph{Survey weights.}
CBS survey weights are intentionally not applied in any outcome regression, so reported rates
and coefficients are unweighted estimates on the analysis sample, not population-representative
statistics. The one exception is internal to constructing the cell-based exposure index.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
%======================================================================

Using Israeli survey data, we ask whether the expansion of remote work coincided with a change in
the motherhood penalty in usual weekly hours. The average change among women observed working is
small, whereas the post-2021 change in the mother/comparison gap increases with an occupation's
calibrated WFH exposure. The strongest contrast is at the top of the exposure distribution and
among mothers with younger children. Those patterns are consistent with greater scheduling
flexibility helping some already employed mothers supply more paid hours.

The result is conditional and does not establish a universal benefit from remote work. The
exposure score partly uses post-period WFH information; the wholly external score yields a weaker
result, pre-trend tests have limited power, and inference varies with the procedure. Employment is
an important separate margin, but its exposure interaction is too imprecise here to settle the
question. The data also contain no earnings amount. Stronger evidence would link hours with
earnings and household time use, measure exposure before treatment in the Israeli setting, and
estimate women's and men's responses together on a design that better separates access to WFH
from occupation choice.
