\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\citet{harrington2025} ask whether the post-pandemic rise of work from home (WFH) reduced the
motherhood penalty in the United States. We take the question to Israel and to the intensive
margin, using Central Bureau of Statistics (CBS) Labor Force Survey microdata on women aged
25--59 in 2017--2019 and 2021--2023. In difference-in-differences (DiD) and triple-differences
(DDD) models of usual weekly hours, with an Israeli-calibrated occupational WFH-exposure index as
the third difference, employed mothers' hours did not change on average ($0.23$ hours, SE $0.18$)
but rose where the work can be done from home: the triple interaction is $3.22$ hours per unit
of exposure (SE $1.02$), $1.71$ hours a week between the mean exposures of the most and least
teleworkable quartiles of occupations. Because exposure varies across only forty occupations we
report three inference procedures: the analytic $p$-value is $0.003$, the permutation $p$-value
$0.032$ and the wild cluster bootstrap $p$-value $0.056$. It survives a saturated fixed-effects
specification, a selection correction and the removal of any single occupation, it is largest
for mothers of children under five, and fathers show the opposite gradient. The employment
margin is estimated too imprecisely to support a comparable conclusion. The result is about
\emph{which} mothers benefit from remote work rather than about mothers as a group.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================

\citet{harrington2025} pose a simple question for the United States: has the rise of work from
home reduced the motherhood penalty in the labor market? Their answer is yes on the extensive
margin and on income: a 10\% rise in WFH raises mothers' employment by roughly $0.78$ percentage
points relative to other women, and employed mothers' incomes rise by about $1.3\%$. This paper
asks the same question of a different country and a different margin. We use Israeli CBS Labor
Force Survey microdata to test whether the post-COVID shift to remote work altered the motherhood
penalty in \emph{usual weekly work hours} among employed women. We also estimate the employment
margin, but for reasons of statistical power documented in Section~\ref{sec:res-extensive} we
treat it as secondary.

The shift to remote work is a rare natural experiment in workplace flexibility, which
\citet{goldin2014} identifies as the last major driver of the remaining gender pay gap. Temporal
and locational flexibility is a good mothers pay for, in lower wages or slower promotion, and a
shock that lowers the cost of supplying it should be detectable in mothers' labor supply, for
mothers already employed most directly in how many hours they supply. Unlike the acute disruption
of 2020, WFH has proven to be a persistent feature of the post-pandemic labor market
\citep{barrero2021}. The stakes are particularly high in Israel, which combines a productive,
internationally integrated labor market with the OECD's highest total fertility rate, $2.87$
children per woman in 2024 against an OECD average of $1.40$ \citep{oecd2025fertility}: an
unusually large share of the female workforce is at any time the mother of a young child, so an
intensive-margin response to flexibility has first-order consequences for aggregate labor input.

Our design exploits the pandemic as the moment at which remote work became a mainstream practice:
in Israel, where the survey began asking only in 2021, between $13\%$ and $16\%$ of employed
women aged 25--59 usually worked from home in 2021--2023. We compare mothers and childless women
across a pre-period of 2017--2019 and a post-period of 2021--2023, excluding 2020
(Section~\ref{sec:data}). A difference-in-differences on this contrast estimates the change in
the hours penalty. A triple-differences model that interacts it with an occupation's WFH exposure
tests whether remote work is the \emph{mechanism}, by asking whether the change is larger in jobs
that can actually be done from home. Its identifying assumption concerns how the motherhood gap
\emph{within} occupations would have evolved across exposure levels absent the shift; pre-period
event studies probe that assumption rather than verify it.

Mothers' hours did not rise on average: the DiD is $0.23$ hours (SE $0.18$), a reasonably precise
null. The effect lives in the interaction with occupational WFH exposure, $3.22$ additional
weekly hours per unit of exposure, or $1.71$ hours between the mean exposures of the top and
bottom quartiles of occupations (a quartile-indicator specification puts the top-versus-bottom
contrast at $1.84$ hours), the same order as the $1.74$-hour intensive-margin penalty itself,
while the implied change at zero exposure is if anything negative. Because the exposure score
varies across only forty occupations, we report three inference procedures, whose $p$-values run
from $0.003$ (analytic) through $0.032$ (permutation) to $0.056$ (wild cluster bootstrap). It
survives the specification, selection, outcome-coding, leave-one-occupation-out and calibration
checks of Section~\ref{sec:results}, mothers did not
sort toward teleworkable occupations after 2021, the gradient is largest for mothers of children
under five, and fathers show the opposite gradient. On the employment margin the DiD is a
precise zero and the DDD is too imprecise to be informative.

\section{Literature Review}\label{sec:lit}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{The motherhood penalty and the flexibility mechanism.}
The child penalty is one of the most robust facts in labor economics. \citet{kleven2019denmark}
show with Danish administrative data that women's earnings fall by roughly 20\% in the long run
relative to men's after a first birth, through reduced hours, exit from the labor market and
moves into family-friendly, lower-paying jobs, and \citet{kleven2019countries} find the pattern
universal across countries and tied to gender norms more than to family policy. Hours are one of
the channels, which is why an intervention that changes the time cost of combining work and care
could move the intensive margin even where it does not move employment. \citet{goldin2014}
locates the remaining gap in the structure of jobs: firms in many occupations pay
disproportionately for long, contiguous, on-site hours, and workers who cannot supply them,
disproportionately mothers, pay a penalty out of proportion to the hours they forgo. Remote work
lowers the cost of supplying a given number of hours, by removing the commute, letting hours be
split around care and permitting a virtual presence in demanding professions. The ingredients
are real: \citet{maspallais2017} show that workers, and women in particular, give up wages for
the option to work from home, and \citet{cortespan2019} show that the skilled gender gap is
concentrated where long hours are rewarded and narrows where substitutes for household time are
cheap. Remote work may also cut the other way, through lower visibility and slower advancement,
so the sign is an empirical question, and Goldin's framework says where to look for it: in the
gradient of the response across occupations by teleworkability, not in its average. For Israel,
\citet{buzaglo2023} shows that within-firm sorting is a first-order driver of the gender wage
gap, a reminder that any occupation-level measure captures only part of the heterogeneity.

\paragraph{What remote work does to hours and to gender gaps.}
Work from home settled at roughly a quarter of paid US workdays after 2021
\citep{barrero2021, barrero2023}, freeing time that workers report splitting between paid work,
care and leisure. The direct evidence on hours points both ways: \citet{gibbs2023} follow
information-technology professionals who moved home in 2020 and find total hours up by roughly
$18\%$ with output flat, while \citet{pabilonia2022} find in American time diaries that on remote
days part of the time saved goes to household production and child care, most of all among
parents. Those two results bracket the question this paper asks of mothers: whether the time
remote work frees is returned to paid hours or absorbed by care. \citet{emanuelharrington2024}
show, for call-centre workers, that who selects into remote work differs sharply from what remote
work does to them, which is why the design below measures \emph{exposure} from occupational
content rather than from realized remote-work status. The experimental and pandemic-period
evidence on gender gaps is mixed: randomly assigned home working raised work supplied without a
fall in output \citep{bloom2015} and randomly assigned flexibility shifted the division of
household tasks toward men \citep{angelici2024}, but in the acute 2020 period women in
WFH-feasible jobs absorbed a larger share of the childcare that school closures created
\citep{adamsprassl2020, alon2020}, and for Israel \citet{yaish2021} document that the 2020
lockdown reallocated paid and unpaid labor within households in gender-unequal ways; this paper
uses post-acute (2021--2023) data rather than the initial-shock period. The paper closest to ours
is \citet{harrington2025}, who show for the United States that the rise of WFH raised mothers'
employment relative to other women's, driven by fields with high returns to hours and inflexible
demands on time, and raised employed mothers' incomes; that second result is an intensive-margin
finding of which hours are one channel, and our hours estimate is its closest analogue, without
treating the two countries or the two outcomes as equivalent. For Israel, \citet{budig2023}
estimate motherhood penalties in employment and earnings that differ across ethno-religious
groups; there is, to our knowledge, no published Israeli estimate of the penalty in hours
conditional on working, so the level we report has no direct domestic benchmark.

\paragraph{Measurement and identification.}
\citet{dingel2020} classify occupations by whether their tasks can be performed entirely at home
and find that 37\% of US jobs can; their classification, mapped to two-digit ISCO-08 codes, is
the starting point for our exposure index, calibrated against realized Israeli behavior
(Section~\ref{sec:exposure}), for which \citet{madhala2020} provide a descriptive baseline.
Because the third difference is a continuous exposure score rather than a treated/untreated
split, the design belongs to the continuous-treatment difference-in-differences family that
\citet{callaway2024} analyze, whose two results for the reading of a single slope are taken up
in Section~\ref{sec:strategy}.

\section{Data and Institutional Context}\label{sec:data}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Source and sample}

The data are individual-level microdata from the Israeli Central Bureau of Statistics Labor Force
Survey for the survey years 2017--2019 and 2021--2023. The analysis sample is women aged 25--59
(CBS age-group codes 3--7). A woman is a \emph{mother} if she has any child under 17 in the
household and childless otherwise; \emph{Post} equals one for survey years 2021 and later. The
comparison group therefore includes women whose children are grown and women who become mothers
within the window (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The year 2020 is excluded because its
arrangements were emergency improvisation rather than the settled remote-work practice whose
effect we want to measure, and because no 2020 CBS extract exists for this project.

The primary outcome is \emph{usual weekly work hours}, recorded by the CBS in bins and assigned
each bin's median (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-data} gives the coding and a 2017 recording correction).
Hours are the only intensive-margin outcome the survey supports: the extract records how a wage
is paid but not its amount. Hours are defined for women who are employed \emph{and} worked in the
survey's reference week, in every year, a harmonization made necessary by a different zero-hours
recording convention in the 2017 extract (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-data}); it makes the hours
population identical across years at the cost of about $10\%$ of each year's employed sample and
narrows the estimand to usual hours among women who were working (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
The secondary outcome is a binary employment indicator (unemployed and not-in-labor-force are
both zero). All regressions
include the same categorical controls: marital status, religion, age group, district of residence
and highest educational attainment (six groups); age enters as the CBS age-group code because the
extract contains no continuous age. CBS survey weights are deliberately not applied in any
outcome regression (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Constructing WFH exposure}\label{sec:exposure}

Realized WFH status is itself a labor-market outcome, jointly determined with hours, so the
mechanism test needs an occupation-level measure of \emph{exposure} to remote work that the
individual does not choose. We build three, each reported.

\emph{External.} The \citet{dingel2020} teleworkability score at the two-digit ISCO-08 level.
This is pre-period by construction, but it is a US task-based measure and misclassifies
occupations where Israeli practice diverged: teaching scores $0.966$ on it, yet Israeli schools
were fully in person throughout the 2022--23 survey years (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-exposure}).

% TODO(merge): "fixed before any estimate is read" is a verifiable claim; cite the dated decision
% note in docs/decisions/ that recorded the rule, or soften to "fixed ex ante" if none exists.
\emph{Calibrated (primary).} The rule that selects the primary measure is fixed before any
estimate is read: the score the headline uses must be anchored on pre-period task content, so
that it cannot be built from the post-2021 hours it is meant to explain, and must not place in the
treated tail occupations that Israeli institutions kept in person. The external index fails the
second condition and the realized index the first; the calibrated index is the only one of the
three that meets both, and the other two are reported as bounds in Section~\ref{sec:res-robust}.
It replaces the external score with the realized 2022--23 Israeli WFH share only where the two
are far apart and the gap is statistically distinguishable from noise; ten of the forty
occupations are swapped, teaching and clerical support among them (rule, scores and fit in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-exposure}). Because the swap test draws on 2022--23 data, inside the
post-period, the calibrated measure is \emph{partly post-treatment}, a documented compromise
rather than a fully pre-treatment index; Section~\ref{sec:res-robust} tests whether the
calibration is what produces the result.

\emph{Realized.} The realized Israeli WFH share by occupation, anchored to 2021. Post-treatment
by construction, it is reported as a bound.

A fourth measure serves three roles. For the employment DDD, occupation is undefined for the
non-employed, so that specification uses a pre-period (2017--2019) shift-share index by
demographic cell (sex, age group, education, district, marital status, religion and continent of
birth; 8,884 cells), equal to the mean of the calibrated occupation score over the cell's
pre-period occupational composition and defined for employed and non-employed women alike. The
same index stratifies the selection correction of Section~\ref{sec:lee}, and because a woman's
post-2021 occupation cannot move it, it also bounds occupational sorting in
Section~\ref{sec:res-ddd-diag}. Its dispersion is a third of the occupation-level index's, so
coefficients on the two are compared per standard deviation, never per unit.

\subsection{The raw gap and its exposure gradient}\label{sec:desc-hours}

Mothers work $38.54$ hours per week before 2021 and $38.84$ after, a gain of $0.31$ hours;
childless women move from $40.04$ to $40.08$. The difference between those two changes,
$0.27$ hours, is the difference-in-differences of equation~\eqref{eq:did} without a single
control, and the regression DiD of Table~\ref{tab:hours}, $0.228$ (SE $0.181$), is within four
hundredths of an hour of it. Mothers remain well below childless women in both periods, and the
raw gap moves by less than a third of an hour across the three pre-period years
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:hours-by-year}), so the groups were not diverging before treatment.

Figure~\ref{fig:dose-response} computes the same difference-in-differences separately within
each quartile of occupational WFH exposure. The bottom three quartiles return $-0.32$, $0.27$ and
$-0.02$ hours, none distinguishable from zero; the top quartile returns $1.55$ hours (95\% CI
$[0.74, 2.36]$). The near-zero average is therefore a weighted average of three nulls and one
substantial positive difference. The figure is a description rather than an independent causal
test: occupational composition and time-varying confounders can differ across quartiles, which
is what the regressions of Section~\ref{sec:results} address. The response is not linear in
exposure: the bottom three quartiles span exposure values of $0.00$ to $0.30$ and the top
quartile $0.30$ to $0.75$, so most of the regressor's dispersion, and the identifying contrast,
sits in one tail.

\section{Empirical Strategy}\label{sec:strategy}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Difference-in-differences}

For outcome $Y_{it}$ (weekly hours conditional on working that week, or employment) we estimate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:did}
Y_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \Mother_i + \beta_2 \Post_t + \beta_3 (\Mother_i \times \Post_t)
+ X'_{it}\gamma + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
where $X_{it}$ is the control vector of Section~\ref{sec:data} and $\beta_3$ is the differential
change in the outcome for mothers relative to childless women after the shift to remote work.

\subsection{Triple differences}

The flexibility framework of \citet{goldin2014} yields one testable hypothesis: after 2021,
mothers' usual hours conditional on working rise relative to childless women's in proportion to
their occupation's WFH exposure, with no response where the work cannot be done from home. The
exposure gradient, not the average change, is the object of interest, so we interact the DiD with
occupational WFH exposure:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ddd}
\begin{split}
Y_{it} = {}& \alpha + \delta_1 \Mother_i + \delta_2 \Post_t + \delta_3 \WFH_{j(i)}
+ \delta_4 (\Mother_i \times \Post_t) \\
& + \delta_5 (\Mother_i \times \WFH_{j(i)}) + \delta_6 (\Post_t \times \WFH_{j(i)}) \\
& + \delta_7 (\Mother_i \times \Post_t \times \WFH_{j(i)}) + X'_{it}\gamma + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $j(i)$ is individual $i$'s occupation. $\delta_7$ asks whether the post-shift change for
mothers relative to non-mothers is larger in more exposed occupations; $\delta_6$ absorbs any
post-2021 change in teleworkable occupations common to mothers and childless women, $\delta_5$
the pre-existing level of the penalty across occupations, and $\delta_4$ is the post-shift change
for mothers at \emph{zero} exposure, not an average effect. Following \citet{olden2022}, the DDD
requires a single parallel-trends assumption: that the mother/non-mother gap in high- versus
low-exposure jobs would have evolved in parallel absent the shift to remote work. For the hours
DDD, $\WFH_j$ is the calibrated occupation-level score and the sample is employed women matched
to a two-digit occupation; for the employment DDD, $\WFH$ is the cell-based index, the sample is
all women, and a $\Mother \times$ age-group interaction is added for the pre-period age imbalance.

Equation~\eqref{eq:ddd} makes each lower-order occupation-level difference linear in exposure,
an unnecessary restriction with forty occupations, and \citet{callaway2024} show that comparing
effects \emph{across} exposure levels, which is what a single slope does, requires the stronger
condition that occupations at different exposure levels would have followed the same path under
any common level of exposure, and that the linear slope is a weighted average of such comparisons
whose weights need not be positive. We therefore estimate two relaxations alongside it. The
\emph{saturated} specification replaces every lower-order term with fixed effects,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ddd-sat}
Y_{it} = \delta_7 (\Mother_i \times \Post_t \times \WFH_{j(i)}) + X'_{it}\gamma
+ \lambda_{j(i),t} + \mu_{j(i),\Mother_i} + \nu_{\Mother_i,t} + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{jt}$, $\mu_{j,m}$ and $\nu_{m,t}$ are occupation-by-year, occupation-by-mother
and mother-by-year effects, which absorb every lower-order term of equation~\eqref{eq:ddd} and
any occupation-specific year shock. The \emph{binned} specification replaces $\WFH_{j(i)}$ with
indicators for the pre-period exposure quartile the occupation falls in, the bins of
Figure~\ref{fig:dose-response}, so that the top-versus-bottom contrast carries a standard error.
For each DDD we also report the minimum detectable effect on the reference distribution the
model's inference uses, $\mathrm{MDE} = \mathrm{SE}\,(t_{0.975} + t_{0.80})$, which is
$2.87 \times \mathrm{SE}$ with 39 degrees of freedom and $2.80 \times \mathrm{SE}$ under the
normal, so that a null can be read as ``genuinely null'' or ``underpowered.''

\subsection{Inference}\label{sec:inference}

The survey is a rotating panel in which most respondents appear about four times, so
equation~\eqref{eq:did} and every descriptive interval are clustered by individual
(Appendix~\ref{sec:app-data} gives the panel counts). For the hours DDD the regressor is constant
within a two-digit occupation, and standard errors are clustered at that level, leaving forty
clusters; the reported $p$-values use a $t$ reference distribution with $G - 1 = 39$ degrees of
freedom and a cluster-count adjustment to the variance. Forty is few enough that we do not rest on
that approximation: for the headline coefficient and for the pre-period coefficients of the
triple-difference event study we also report wild cluster bootstrap $p$-values, with Rademacher
weights, the null imposed and $9{,}999$ draws \citep{roodman2019, fischer2021}, and
Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics} adds a permutation test that relies on no asymptotic approximation
at all. The joint pre-period test remains the analytic Wald $F$, since a joint bootstrap test is
not available in the implementation used. The employment DDD is clustered on the
age-by-education-by-district cell (about 210 clusters).

\subsection{Selection into employment: Lee bounds}\label{sec:lee}

Hours are observed only for women who are working, and working is itself an outcome of the
same treatment. We therefore report trimming bounds in the spirit of \citet{lee2009}: the share
of post-period mothers observed with hours in excess of a parallel-trends counterfactual is
trimmed from the top of that cell's hours distribution for a lower bound and from the bottom for
an upper bound, the model is refit, and the \citet{imbens2004} interval for the identified set
is reported. For the DDD the trimming is done within quartiles of the cell-based index, which is
defined for the non-employed. Appendix~\ref{sec:app-lee} gives the construction and its
assumptions.

\subsection{Diagnostics, falsification, and the fathers' comparison}\label{sec:diagnostics}

We estimate event-study versions of equation~\eqref{eq:did} with $\Mother \times$ year
interactions (reference year 2019) for both outcomes and test the pre-period coefficients
jointly; because those test the difference-in-differences assumption rather than the
triple-differences one, we also estimate the event-study version of equation~\eqref{eq:ddd}
itself, with $\Post$ replaced by survey-year interactions at every level, and apply the same
joint test to its pre-period coefficients.

The falsification test is a permutation test on the exposure score. Under the sharp null that
teleworkability is unrelated to the post-2021 change in the motherhood gap, the forty occupation
scores are exchangeable, so we reassign them at random across the forty occupation codes $999$
times, holding every woman's occupation and the mother/period structure fixed, refit
equation~\eqref{eq:ddd}, and record the cluster-robust $t$-statistic of the triple interaction;
the permutation $p$-value is the share of draws, counting the observed one, whose $|t|$ reaches
the observed value \citep{phipson2010}. Because occupation is observed when the outcome is, we
also check whether remote work drew mothers into teleworkable occupations after 2021, with a
difference-in-differences that takes the exposure score itself as the outcome, and bound the
channel by re-estimating the hours DDD on the pre-period cell index of
Section~\ref{sec:exposure}, which a woman's later occupation cannot move.

The remaining checks, collected in Table~\ref{tab:robust}, re-estimate the hours DDD with the
exposure measure, the age control, the sample, the outcome coding and the inference procedure
varied in turn, and re-estimate it by the age of the youngest child, separately for Jewish and
Arab women, and on the male subsample with ``mother'' read as ``father.'' The last is a
comparison, not a placebo: fathers in teleworkable occupations are exposed to remote work exactly
as mothers are, so a negative gradient would be evidence on household reallocation rather than on
a confound. Coefficients across disjoint subsamples are compared with two-sample $z$-tests.

\subsection{The hours DDD: the paper's headline result}\label{sec:res-ddd}

Column~(2) of Table~\ref{tab:hours} interacts the DiD with the calibrated occupation-level
exposure index. The triple interaction is $3.224$\sym{**} (SE $1.022$, $N = 246{,}326$): among
working mothers, those in more WFH-exposed occupations saw a larger post-2021 increase in weekly
hours relative to childless women in the same occupations. The index has a standard deviation of
$0.199$, so the estimate is $0.64$ hours per standard deviation, and between the quartiles of
Figure~\ref{fig:dose-response}, whose mean exposures are $0.06$ and $0.59$, the regression
implies $1.71$ hours a week against $1.87$ from the raw cell means. At zero exposure the implied
change for mothers is $-0.539$ (SE $0.336$), and the gradient is net of whatever happened to
hours in teleworkable occupations for all women. The estimate is $3.15$ analytic standard errors
from zero, qualified by the bootstrap and permutation $p$-values of Section~\ref{sec:res-robust};
the minimum detectable effect on the $t_{39}$ reference is $2.94$ hours per unit, or $0.58$ per
standard deviation, so an effect of this size is detectable without much room to spare.

Columns~(3) and~(4) relax the functional form. With occupation-by-year, occupation-by-mother and
mother-by-year fixed effects in place of every lower-order term (equation~\eqref{eq:ddd-sat}),
the triple interaction is $2.876$ (SE $0.950$), a tenth below the pooled estimate and still
significant at the $1\%$ level on the analytic standard error. Column~(4) replaces the score with
indicators for the pre-period exposure quartile: relative to the least teleworkable quartile, the
post-2021 change in the gap is $0.753$ hours (SE $0.483$) in the second quartile, $0.600$
(SE $0.485$) in the third and $1.844$ (SE $0.595$, $p < 0.01$) in the top quartile, the only
contrast that is individually significant. The response is a step at the top of the exposure
distribution more than a smooth gradient, and that top quartile holds six of the forty
occupations; the pooled and saturated columns, which use all forty, agree with it, and no single
occupation drives it (Section~\ref{sec:res-robust}).

\subsection{Diagnostics for the triple difference}\label{sec:res-ddd-diag}

\paragraph{Pre-trends.}
Panel~(b) of Figure~\ref{fig:pretrend} reports the event-study analogue of
equation~\eqref{eq:ddd}, the test the triple difference itself requires
(Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}), on the same sample, index and clustering as column~(2) of
Table~\ref{tab:hours}. Neither pre-period coefficient is close to significant or far from zero,
the joint Wald test does not come near rejecting ($F(2, 39) = 0.041$, $p = 0.960$, wild cluster
bootstrap $p = 0.85$ on their sum), and the post-period path steps up in 2021 and rises:
$3.011$\sym{*}, $3.545$\sym{**}, $4.020$\sym{**}, all straddling the pooled estimate. The test
has limited power: with forty clusters the pre-period standard errors ($2.24$ and $1.74$) are of
the same order as the effect, so each pre-period interval contains values as large as the
estimate itself. The test shows no sign of a violation; it does not rule one out.

\paragraph{Selection.}
Trimming the excess selection among post-period mothers within each quartile of the cell-based
index (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-lee}) gives bounds of $3.126$ (SE $1.144$) and $3.443$
(SE $1.022$); the Imbens--Manski 95\% interval, $[1.021, 5.324]$, excludes zero.

\paragraph{Sorting across occupations.}
Selection across occupations within the sample is the remaining channel, and the exposure score
taken as an outcome does not show it: on the DDD's own sample, a difference-in-differences with
the occupation-level score as the dependent variable gives $\Mother \times \Post = 0.0013$
(SE $0.0035$), under one hundredth of the score's standard deviation, the probability of being in
the top quartile moves by $0.4$ percentage points (SE $0.7$), and the event-study version is flat
in every year (Table~\ref{tab:robust}, \emph{Occupational sorting}). The bound that does not
depend on the current occupation, the hours DDD on the pre-period demographic-cell index, is
$0.139$ hours per standard deviation of that index (SE $0.159$, 245 clusters) against $0.640$ per
standard deviation of the occupation score; the cell index is a far coarser measure of a woman's
own teleworkability, so the bound is positive and imprecise, and the sorting DiD is the
informative test.

\subsection{Robustness}\label{sec:res-robust}

Table~\ref{tab:robust} collects the remaining checks.

\paragraph{Exposure measure.}
With the raw \citet{dingel2020} external index in place of the calibrated one the triple
interaction is $0.672$ (SE $0.884$), positive but insignificant; with the realized 2021-anchored
index it is $5.195$\sym{*} (SE $2.363$). The external index is the only one built entirely from
pre-treatment information and it yields the weakest estimate, so the natural objection is that
calibrating on 2022--23 realized WFH lets the regressor absorb post-period outcomes. Three checks
address it while holding the sample at all forty occupations. Calibrating on men only, so that no
woman in the regression contributes to her own regressor, swaps twelve occupations rather than
ten and gives $3.869$\sym{***} (SE $0.979$). Keeping the external score as the regressor but
letting the ten swapped occupations carry their own $\Mother \times \Post$ change gives an
external-score gradient of $2.339$ (SE $1.352$, $p = 0.09$) beside a negative change for the
swapped group ($-1.347$, SE $0.926$): the occupations the external index places in the treated
tail did not respond, and once they are allowed not to, the gradient in the external score
re-emerges at two thirds of the headline. Dropping them altogether, so that the two indices
coincide on the thirty occupations that remain, gives $4.309$\sym{**} (SE $1.192$, 30 clusters).
The calibration does not produce the result, but it cannot be made into a pre-treatment measure,
and a reader who weights the external index above the others will read the headline as
directionally supported rather than established.

\paragraph{Covariate balance and age.}
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:balance-quartile} reports the pre-period difference between mothers and
childless women in every control within each exposure quartile of the estimation sample, and the
differences vary with exposure: mothers are younger than childless women by $0.54$ of an
age-group code in the least teleworkable quartile and older by $0.08$ in the most, the
academic-degree gap falls from $10.8$ percentage points to under $2$, and the marriage gap rises
from $32$ to $43$ points. A gap that itself varies with exposure is what the triple interaction
is identified off, so two specifications relax the age control. A $\Mother \times$ age-group
interaction leaves the estimate essentially unchanged ($3.205$\sym{**}, SE $1.055$); reweighting
to balance pre-period age groups attenuates it by about 19\% to $2.597$\sym{*} (SE $1.050$),
still significant at the 5\% level but below the design's minimum detectable effect, the largest
single move any check produces.

\paragraph{Sample.}
Excluding the 2023 survey year, whose final quarter includes the start of the October 2023 war
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), the triple interaction is $2.979$\sym{*} (SE $1.170$), smaller
because 2023 carries the largest event-study coefficient but still significant at the 5\% level,
while the plain hours DiD stays a null ($0.071$, SE $0.198$). Removing each of the forty
occupations in turn (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:loo}) leaves the estimate between $2.415$, without
science and engineering professionals, and $3.580$, without business and administration
associate professionals; the largest move is $0.79$ of the headline standard error, no refit
leaves the band of one standard error around the headline, and every refit is significant at the
5\% level on its own analytic standard error ($p \leq 0.017$).

\paragraph{Outcome coding.}
The hours variable is a bin-median approximation and the effect is smaller than one bin width,
so the result should not depend on how the bins are turned into hours. Dropping the two imputed
irregular-hours codes gives $3.056$\sym{**} (SE $1.059$). Because 35 and 40 hours are exact bin
edges, the same specification can be estimated as a linear probability model: the probability of
usually working at least 35 hours rises by $0.096$\sym{**} (SE $0.029$) per unit of exposure for
mothers relative to childless women after 2021, and of at least 40 hours by $0.107$\sym{**}
(SE $0.038$), or $1.9$ and $2.1$ percentage points per standard deviation against pre-period
shares of $71\%$ and $61\%$. The effect is a shift of mothers in teleworkable jobs across the
full-time boundary.

\paragraph{Inference and falsification.}
Clustering two ways, by individual and by occupation, leaves the headline standard error
unchanged at $1.022$. The two procedures that address the forty-cluster problem disagree with
the analytic $p$-value of $0.003$: the wild cluster bootstrap gives $p = 0.056$, with a bootstrap
95\% confidence set of $[-0.26, 5.84]$, and the permutation test gives $p = 0.032$ (Monte-Carlo
standard error $0.006$): $31$ of $999$ random reassignments of the forty scores across
occupations produce a $|t|$ at least as large as the observed $3.15$ (Appendix
Figure~\ref{fig:permutation}). A design with this cluster structure produces an exposure gradient
as large as ours about three times in a hundred when exposure carries no information, which is
the falsification a placebo should supply and a fathers' regression, a different and also-treated
population, cannot. The estimate is significant at the $1\%$, $5\%$ and $10\%$ levels under the
three procedures, and we report all three rather than choosing among them.

\subsection{Heterogeneity by the age of the youngest child}\label{sec:res-childage}

If remote work relaxes a time constraint that binds through child care, the response should be
largest for mothers of young children and smallest for mothers of teenagers.
Table~\ref{tab:childage} re-estimates the hours DiD and DDD four times, each comparing the
mothers whose youngest child is aged 0--4, 5--9, 10--14 or 15--17 with the same childless women.
The average effect is a null in every group, and the exposure gradient declines monotonically
with the child's age: $3.656$\sym{**} hours per unit of exposure (SE $1.079$) for mothers of
under-fives, $3.211$\sym{*} (SE $1.311$) for 5--9, $2.741$ (SE $1.525$) for 10--14 and $1.862$
(SE $0.935$) for 15--17, half the under-five estimate. The four regressions share the control
group, so they are not independent samples and their intervals overlap; the pattern is a gradient
consistent with the mechanism rather than a set of statistically separated effects, and the
estimate is not carried by mothers of teenagers.

\subsection{Heterogeneity by population group, and the fathers' comparison}\label{sec:res-subgroup}

Table~\ref{tab:subgroup} re-estimates the hours DiD and DDD for Jewish and Arab women and for
men. For Jewish women the pattern is the pooled pattern: a DiD of $0.302$ (SE $0.203$) and a DDD
of $2.847$\sym{**} (SE $0.913$). For Arab women the DiD is $-0.072$ (SE $0.479$) and the DDD
$5.507$\sym{*} (SE $2.674$), nominally twice the Jewish estimate but statistically
indistinguishable from it ($z = 0.942$, $p = 0.35$) and resting on a subsample nine times smaller;
it is imprecise rather than a documented heterogeneous effect.

Re-estimating the same models on men, with ``mother'' read as ``father,'' gives a DiD of
$-0.400$\sym{*} (SE $0.188$) and a DDD of $-1.762$ (SE $1.015$, $p = 0.09$). Fathers are exposed
to remote work as mothers are (Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}), so a null was never the
prediction; what the comparison shows is that the exposure gradient has the opposite sign for
fathers, and that the two gradients are distinguishable ($z = 3.461$, $p = 0.0005$). A macro
trend common to all workers in teleworkable jobs cannot produce a positive gradient for mothers
beside a negative one for fathers, and a negative gradient for fathers is what within-household
reallocation would look like if mothers' additional paid hours were matched by fathers'
additional care hours. We report that as a reading the data are consistent with, not as a result
the design identifies.

\subsection{The extensive margin, briefly}\label{sec:res-extensive}

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:extensive} reports equation~\eqref{eq:did} with employment as the
outcome: $\Mother \times \Post = -0.0053$ (SE $0.0052$), an informative null with a standard
error of half a percentage point, whose pre-period event-study coefficients pass their joint test
($F(2, 79{,}069) = 0.63$, $p = 0.53$). The employment DDD with the cell-based index is
$-0.0257$ (SE $0.0725$), a null of a different kind: the design could detect a triple interaction
no smaller than $1.43$ percentage points per standard deviation of the cell index, or $1.85\%$ of
the baseline employment rate, and against \citeauthor{harrington2025}'s $0.78$ percentage points
per 10\% rise in WFH it is underpowered by a factor of two to three. The shortfall is structural,
a binary outcome and a regressor with a standard deviation of $0.07$, and re-clustering,
combining the two margins and refining the cell partition did not close it. The employment DDD is
therefore \emph{uninformative}, evidence neither for nor against a WFH-mediated employment
effect, and the two nulls should not be read as one verdict.

%======================================================================
\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
%======================================================================

Among mothers who remain in work there is no post-2021 hours effect on average, and a large one
in teleworkable occupations: a triple interaction of about $3.2$ hours per unit of exposure and
an implied change at zero exposure that is if anything negative. This is consistent with WFH access relaxing a binding time constraint for
mothers in teleworkable jobs, the \citet{goldin2014} mechanism, and hard to reconcile with a
general recovery common to all women, which would have raised hours across occupations rather
than only where the work can be done from home. What makes the finding credible is the pattern of
evidence rather than any one $p$-value: a saturated specification that changes nothing, a
top-quartile contrast significant on its own, a leave-one-out range that never leaves the
headline's standard error, a monotone child-age gradient, an opposite-signed fathers' gradient and
a selection correction that barely moves the bounds. The gradient is identified off a concrete
contrast: ICT professionals stayed high on every exposure measure, while teaching and clerical
support, which Israeli practice kept in person, moved sharply down.

The magnitude is economically meaningful where it applies. For a mother at the top quartile's
mean exposure the regression implies a post-2021 change of about $1.4$ hours a week
($-0.54 + 3.22 \times 0.59$), and the raw top-quartile difference-in-differences of
Figure~\ref{fig:dose-response} is $1.55$. The penalty it is set against must be the penalty in
those same jobs, not the average one: column~(2) of Table~\ref{tab:hours} puts the pre-period
motherhood gap at $-1.45 - 1.30 \times 0.59 \approx -2.2$ hours at that exposure, so mothers in
the most teleworkable jobs recovered roughly three fifths of their penalty after 2021. At the
bottom quartile's mean exposure of $0.06$ the implied change is $-0.35$ hours: mothers in the
least teleworkable jobs recovered none of it. The size matches the commuting arithmetic:
\citet{aksoy2023} put the time saved at about 72 minutes per day worked from home, so two remote
days a week free roughly two and a half hours, and a mother who returns most of that time to
paid work is a mother whose hours rise by about the amount estimated here. The timing is
consistent with a multi-year adjustment: the DDD event study rises from $3.0$ in 2021 to $4.0$
in 2023 (on the 2023 year, see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

A general recovery is not the only alternative to that mechanism; three demand-side accounts
also fit a gradient by teleworkability. Employers in professional and ICT occupations may have
changed the contracts they offer mothers after 2021, with no change in mothers' constraints; the
saturated specification absorbs any occupation-by-year shock common to mothers and childless
women, so this needs a change aimed at mothers in teleworkable occupations specifically. The
2021--22 technology hiring boom raised hours in exposed occupations for everyone; the fathers'
opposite-signed gradient rules out a shock common to all workers in those occupations, but not
one that fell on mothers alone. And usual hours are self-reported: if mothers working from home
report the hours they are available while fathers report the hours they work, the two gradients
would diverge as observed with no change in labor supplied; the child-age gradient weighs against
this, since a reporting difference has no reason to be twice as large for mothers of infants as
for mothers of teenagers. None of the three is excluded by the design, and we read the exposure
gradient as the time-constraint mechanism's signature rather than its proof.

Why hours and not employment? The employment DDD is not a failed test of the mechanism but an
uninformative one (Section~\ref{sec:res-extensive}). Substantively the two results are coherent:
the effect operates on \emph{how much} mothers in teleworkable jobs work, not on \emph{whether}
they are employed, which is the margin \citet{kleven2019denmark} identify as a leading channel of
the child penalty, and it is where our findings depart from \citet{harrington2025}, whose US
evidence is on employment and income. The policy reading is correspondingly narrow: WFH
availability matters for how intensively mothers who already hold \emph{teleworkable} jobs can
work, not for whether mothers in general hold jobs.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{Identification and exposure.}
The triple difference's own pre-trend test (Section~\ref{sec:res-ddd-diag}) shows no visible
violation but lacks the power to rule one out. The exposure measure that delivers the headline
draws on post-period Israeli data for its calibration, and although the checks of
Section~\ref{sec:res-robust} show the calibration does not produce the result, the fully
pre-treatment external index yields a positive and insignificant estimate. The sorting check finds no post-2021 movement of
mothers across exposure levels but bounds the effect only imprecisely on a regressor fixed before
treatment. Exposure is assigned at the two-digit occupation level, forty groups, whereas
\citet{buzaglo2023} shows that within-firm sorting matters as much as occupation for Israeli
gender gaps; within-occupation heterogeneity in teleworkability is classical measurement error in
the regressor and attenuates the triple interaction toward zero, and the calibrated score mixes
two scales (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-exposure}). Occupation codes masked by the CBS for
disclosure control are dropped when building exposure; a proxy check finds no strong evidence of
bias from this. How precisely the headline is estimated is the range of the three inference
procedures in Section~\ref{sec:res-robust}, not the analytic $p$-value.

\paragraph{The narrowed hours estimand.}
Hours are usual hours among women who were working, not among all women holding a job, and
roughly $10\%$ of each year's employed sample is excluded on that basis. The exclusion is not
innocuous for the triple interaction because absence is related to exposure: it is highest in
the least teleworkable quartile, and among mothers it fell after 2021 in the top quartile while
rising in the bottom one (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-data}). The bounds of Section~\ref{sec:lee}
therefore define selection on the observed-hours sample; they handle only \emph{excess}
selection under monotonicity and found little to correct, which is not the same as the concern
being absent.

\paragraph{Sample and period.}
The 2023 coefficient is the largest in both event studies, and the 2023 survey year includes the
quarter in which the war that began on 7 October 2023 disrupted the labor market: reserve
call-ups, which bear on the fathers' comparison, and school closures and evacuations, which bear
on mothers' hours. The estimate survives dropping the year and 2021 and 2022 are individually
significant on the triple difference, but the 2023 point should be held more loosely. Early 2021
likewise included a third lockdown, so the post-period is ``post-acute'' more cleanly in its
later years than in its first. The control group is any woman aged 25--59 with no child under 17
in the household, so it includes women whose children are grown and women who become mothers
within the window, as in \citet{harrington2025}; if part of it responds to the treatment as
mothers do, the DiD is biased toward zero. Age enters as a categorical code because the extract
has no continuous age, and mothers and non-mothers are imbalanced on it in a way that tracks
exposure (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:balance-quartile}); the age-interacted and reweighted
specifications of Section~\ref{sec:res-robust} address this and the estimate survives both.
% The exclusion is of the whole year: the survey-month field (ChodeshSeker) is dropped at cleaning,
% so a fourth-quarter-only exclusion would need a schema change (audit item I5).

\paragraph{Survey weights.}
CBS survey weights are intentionally not applied in any outcome regression; reported rates and
coefficients are unweighted estimates on the analysis sample, not population-representative
statistics. The one exception is internal to constructing the cell-based exposure index.

intensively mothers already employed in teleworkable jobs work, not on whether mothers are
