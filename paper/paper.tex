\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\citet{harrington2025} ask whether the post-pandemic rise of work from home (WFH) reduced the
motherhood penalty in the United States. We take the question to Israel and to the intensive
margin, using Central Bureau of Statistics (CBS) Labor Force Survey microdata on women aged
25--59 in 2017--2019 and 2021--2023. In difference-in-differences (DiD) and triple-differences
(DDD) models of usual weekly hours, with an Israeli-calibrated occupational WFH-exposure index as
the third difference, employed mothers' hours did not change on average ($0.23$ hours, SE $0.18$)
but rose where the work can be done from home: the triple interaction is $3.22$ hours per unit
of exposure (SE $1.02$), about $1.8$ hours a week between the most and least teleworkable
quartiles of occupations, with no gain at zero exposure. The estimate is significant at the
$1\%$ level on the analytic standard error, at $5\%$ under a permutation test and at $10\%$
under a wild cluster bootstrap. It survives a saturated fixed-effects specification, a selection
correction and the removal of any single occupation, and it is largest for mothers of children
under five; fathers show the opposite gradient. The result is about \emph{which} mothers benefit
from remote work rather than about mothers as a group.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================

\citet{harrington2025} pose a simple question for the United States: has the rise of work from
home reduced the motherhood penalty in the labor market? Their answer is yes on the extensive
margin and on income: a 10\% rise in WFH raises mothers' employment by roughly $0.78$ percentage
points relative to other women, and employed mothers' incomes rise by about $1.3\%$. This paper
asks the same question of a different country and a different margin. We use Israeli CBS Labor
Force Survey microdata to test whether the post-COVID shift to remote work altered the motherhood
penalty in \emph{usual weekly work hours} among employed women. We also estimate the employment
margin, but for reasons of statistical power documented in Section~\ref{sec:res-extensive} we
treat it as secondary.

The shift to remote work is a rare natural experiment in workplace flexibility, which
\citet{goldin2014} identifies as the last major driver of the remaining gender pay gap. Temporal
and locational flexibility is a good mothers pay for, in lower wages or slower promotion, and a
shock that lowers the cost of supplying it should be detectable in mothers' labor supply --- for
mothers already employed, most directly in how many hours they supply. Unlike the acute disruption
of 2020, WFH has proven to be a persistent feature of the post-pandemic labor market
\citep{barrero2021}, which makes the question policy-relevant rather than merely historical.

The stakes are particularly high in Israel, which combines a productive, internationally
integrated labor market with the OECD's highest total fertility rate, $2.87$ children per woman
in 2024 against an OECD average of $1.40$ \citep{oecd2025fertility}: an unusually large share of
the female workforce is at any time the mother of a young child, so an intensive-margin response
to flexibility has first-order consequences for aggregate labor input. Israeli practice also
diverges from US task-based predictions of which jobs can be done remotely; teaching returned
fully to the classroom once schools reopened, whatever its task content says, which matters for
how ``WFH exposure'' should be measured.

Our design exploits the pandemic as the moment at which remote work became a mainstream practice.
In the United States, working from home rose from roughly 5\% of paid workdays before 2020 to
25--30\% afterward \citep{barrero2021}; in Israel, where the survey began asking only in 2021,
between $13\%$ and $16\%$ of employed women aged 25--59 usually worked from home in 2021--2023.
We compare mothers and childless women across a pre-period of 2017--2019 and a post-period of
2021--2023, excluding 2020 (Section~\ref{sec:data}). A difference-in-differences on this contrast
estimates the change in the hours penalty. A triple-differences model that interacts it with an
occupation's WFH exposure tests whether remote work is the \emph{mechanism}, by asking whether the
change is larger in jobs that can actually be done from home.

Mothers' hours did not rise on average: the DiD is $0.23$ hours (SE $0.18$), a reasonably precise
null. The effect lives in the interaction with occupational WFH exposure, $3.22$ additional
weekly hours per unit of exposure, or $1.8$ hours between the top and bottom quartiles of
occupations, the same order as the $1.74$-hour intensive-margin penalty itself, while the implied
change at zero exposure is if anything negative. Because the exposure score varies across only
forty occupations, we report three inference procedures that place the estimate at the $1\%$,
$5\%$ and $10\%$ levels. It survives a saturated fixed-effects specification, a selection
correction, alternative codings of the outcome, the removal of any one occupation, and the
exclusion of the occupations whose exposure was calibrated on post-period data; a check on
occupational sorting finds no post-2021 movement of mothers toward teleworkable occupations;
the gradient is largest for mothers of children under five; and fathers show the opposite
gradient. On the employment margin the DiD is a precise zero and the DDD is too imprecise to be
informative.

Section~\ref{sec:lit} places the question in the literature; Section~\ref{sec:data} describes
the data and the exposure measures; Section~\ref{sec:descriptives} shows the hours margin before
any model is imposed on it; Section~\ref{sec:strategy} sets out the specifications, inference and
selection correction; Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results;
Sections~\ref{sec:discussion}--\ref{sec:limitations} interpret them; and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Literature Review}\label{sec:lit}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{The motherhood penalty.}
The child penalty is one of the most robust facts in labor economics. \citet{kleven2019denmark}
use Danish administrative data and an event study around the first birth to show that women's
earnings fall by roughly 20\% in the long run relative to men's, through reduced hours, exit
from the labor market and moves into family-friendly, lower-paying jobs; \citet{kleven2019countries}
find the pattern universal across countries and its magnitude tied to gender norms more than to
family policy. Hours are one of the channels, which is why an intervention that changes the time
cost of combining work and care could move the intensive margin even where it does not move
employment.

\paragraph{Temporal flexibility as the mechanism.}
\citet{goldin2014} argues that the last chapter of gender convergence turns on the structure of
jobs rather than on human capital. In many occupations firms pay disproportionately for long,
contiguous, on-site hours, and workers who cannot supply them, disproportionately mothers, pay a
penalty out of proportion to the hours they forgo. Remote work lowers the cost of supplying a
given number of hours: it removes the commute, lets hours be split around care obligations, and
permits a virtual presence in demanding professions. The prediction for the intensive margin is
direct: mothers in occupations where the work can be done from home should supply more hours
after the shift, relative to childless women in the same occupations, and mothers in occupations
that cannot should not. The ingredients are real: \citet{maspallais2017} show that workers, and
women in particular, give up wages for the option to work from home, and \citet{cortespan2019}
show that the skilled gender gap is concentrated where long hours are rewarded and narrows where
substitutes for household time are cheap. Remote work may also cut the other way, through lower
visibility and slower advancement, so the sign is an empirical question, and Goldin's framework
says where to look for it: in the gradient of the response across occupations by teleworkability,
not in its average.

\paragraph{Does remote work narrow the gap in practice?}
The evidence on whether flexible arrangements realize this potential is mixed. \citet{bloom2015}
find that randomly assigned home working raised work supplied without a fall in output, and
\citet{angelici2024} that randomly assigned flexibility shifted the division of household tasks
and raised women's well-being. The acute pandemic period is more ambiguous: \citet{adamsprassl2020}
show that WFH-feasible jobs were better shielded from the 2020 shock but that women in them
absorbed a larger share of the childcare created by school closures \citep[see also][]{alon2020},
and for Israel \citet{yaish2021} document that the 2020 lockdown reallocated paid and unpaid
labor within households in gender-unequal ways. This paper uses post-acute (2021--2023) data
rather than the initial-shock period most of this literature studies.

\paragraph{Remote work and hours.}
The evidence closest to our outcome concerns what remote work does to hours themselves.
\citet{barrero2023} document that work from home settled at roughly a quarter of paid workdays in
the United States after 2021 and that the time it frees is, in workers' own accounts, split between
paid work, care and leisure. Two studies identify the direction of the hours response.
\citet{gibbs2023} follow the personnel records of information-technology professionals who moved
home in 2020 and find total hours up by roughly $18\%$ with output flat, so hours rose even as
output per hour fell; \citet{pabilonia2022} use American time diaries and find that on remote days
part of the time saved goes to household production and child care rather than to paid work,
with the largest reallocation among parents. Those two results bracket the question this paper
asks of mothers: whether the time remote work frees is returned to paid hours or absorbed by
care. \citet{emanuelharrington2024} show, for call-centre workers, that who selects into remote
work differs sharply from what remote work does to them, which is why the design below measures
\emph{exposure} from occupational content rather than from realized remote-work status. For
Israel, \citet{budig2023} estimate motherhood penalties in employment and earnings that differ
across ethno-religious groups; there is, to our knowledge, no published Israeli estimate of the
penalty in hours conditional on working, so the level we report in Section~\ref{sec:results} has
no direct domestic benchmark.

\paragraph{Measuring who can work from home.}
\citet{dingel2020} classify occupations by whether their tasks can be performed entirely at home
and find that 37\% of US jobs can. Their classification, mapped to two-digit ISCO-08 codes, is
the starting point for our exposure index; because a US task-based measure misclassifies
occupations where Israeli practice diverged, we calibrate it against realized Israeli behavior
(Section~\ref{sec:exposure}), for which \citet{madhala2020} provide a descriptive baseline. Any
occupation-level measure captures only part of the relevant heterogeneity: \citet{buzaglo2023}
shows that within-firm sorting is a first-order driver of the Israeli gender wage gap.

\paragraph{Remote work and the motherhood penalty.}
The paper closest to ours is \citet{harrington2025}, who show for the United States that the rise
of WFH raised mothers' employment relative to other women's, driven by fields with high returns to
hours and inflexible demands on time, and raised employed mothers' incomes by about $1.3\%$. That
second result is an intensive-margin finding of which hours are one channel; our hours estimate
is its closest analogue. We take the question to Israel, to hours conditional on working, with an
explicit correction for the selection that conditioning on employment creates, and to an exposure
measure calibrated to Israeli practice rather than imported from US task content.

\paragraph{Identifying a dose-response.}
Because the third difference is a continuous exposure score rather than a treated/untreated
split, the design belongs to the continuous-treatment difference-in-differences family that
\citet{callaway2024} analyze. Two of their results shape the reading below. Comparing effects
\emph{across} exposure levels, which is what a single slope does, requires the stronger condition
that occupations at different exposure levels would have followed the same path under any common
level of exposure; and the linear specification returns a weighted average of such comparisons
whose weights need not be positive. We therefore report the triple interaction three ways: as the
linear slope, under a saturated specification that absorbs every occupation-level margin except
the triple interaction, and as quartile contrasts on the bins of the descriptive dose-response
figure (Section~\ref{sec:strategy}).

\section{Data and Institutional Context}\label{sec:data}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Source and sample}

The data are individual-level microdata from the Israeli Central Bureau of Statistics Labor Force
Survey for the survey years 2017--2019 and 2021--2023. The analysis sample is women aged 25--59
(CBS age-group codes 3--7). A woman is a \emph{mother} if she has any child under 17 in the
household and childless otherwise; \emph{Post} equals one for survey years 2021 and later. The
year 2020 is excluded because its arrangements were emergency improvisation rather than the
settled remote-work practice whose effect we want to measure, and because no 2020 CBS extract
exists for this project, a constraint that also shapes the exposure index
(Section~\ref{sec:exposure}).

The primary outcome is \emph{usual weekly work hours}. The CBS records usual hours in bins; we
assign each bin its median ($4$, $11$, $18$, $25.5$, $32$, $37$, $42$, $47$, $54.5$ and $78.5$
hours) and impute the two irregular-hours codes from observed medians within the same period.
Mid-distribution bins are about five hours wide, so an effect smaller than one bin width, such as
the headline estimate below, is best read as a shift in the share of women crossing a bin
boundary. Hours are the only intensive-margin outcome the survey supports: the extract records
how a wage is paid but not its amount. Hours are defined for women who are employed \emph{and}
worked in the survey's reference week, a restriction that changes the estimand. In the 2017 file,
employed respondents absent from work in the reference week had their usual hours recorded as
zero, whereas from 2018 onward the same respondents were assigned a real value; left uncorrected,
this enters the pre-period as roughly $5{,}200$ spurious zeros, and because absence is more
common among mothers ($12.4\%$ of employed mothers against $7.3\%$ of childless women) they fall
unevenly across the comparison this paper makes. Defining hours on the reference-week workers in
\emph{every} year costs about $10\%$ of each year's employed sample, makes the hours population
identical across years, and narrows the estimand to usual hours among women who were working
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

The secondary outcome is a binary employment indicator, equal to one if the respondent reports
being employed and zero otherwise (unemployed and not-in-labor-force are both zero).

All regressions include the same categorical controls: marital status, religion, age group,
district of residence and highest educational attainment (six groups). Age enters as the CBS
age-group code because the extract contains no continuous age. CBS survey weights are
deliberately not applied in any outcome regression (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Constructing WFH exposure}\label{sec:exposure}

Realized WFH status is itself a labor-market outcome, jointly determined with hours, so the
mechanism test needs an occupation-level measure of \emph{exposure} to remote work that the
individual does not choose. We build it in three steps, each producing a measure that is
reported.

\emph{External.} The \citet{dingel2020} teleworkability score, via an O*NET/SOC-to-ISCO crosswalk,
at the two-digit ISCO-08 level. This is pre-period by construction, but it is a US task-based
measure and misclassifies occupations where Israeli practice diverged. Teaching (ISCO 23) is the
clearest case: it scores $0.966$ on the external index, yet Israeli schools, closed and reopened
in stages through 2020 and early 2021 \citep{somekh2021, hale2021}, were fully in person
throughout the 2022--23 survey years, and teachers did not work from home. The crosswalk that
maps the US occupation codes onto ISCO-08 is not documented in the replication materials, so a
reader can check the forty resulting scores (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:exposure-scores}) but not
rebuild them from the source.

\emph{Calibrated (primary).} The rule that selects the primary measure is fixed before any
estimate is read: the score the headline uses must be anchored on pre-period task content, so
that it cannot be built from the post-2021 hours it is meant to explain, and must not place in the
treated tail occupations that Israeli institutions kept in person. The external index fails the
second condition and the realized index the first; the calibrated index is the only one of the
three that meets both, and the other two are reported as bounds in Section~\ref{sec:res-robust}.
We correct the external score only where the gap between it and the realized 2022--23 Israeli WFH
share is large (greater than $0.5$) \emph{and} distinguishable from sampling noise by a one-sided
test at the 95\% level with a standard error clustered by individual. Ten of the forty two-digit
occupations are swapped to their realized values: teaching falls from $0.966$ to $0.069$,
clerical support (ISCO 41) from $1.000$ to $0.098$, ICT professionals (ISCO 25) from $1.000$ to
$0.360$. Because the swap test draws on 2022--23 data, inside the post-period, the calibrated
measure is a documented compromise rather than a fully pre-treatment index; the realized shares
are computed on men and women together, and Section~\ref{sec:res-robust} re-derives them from men
alone.

Across the forty occupations, on men and women pooled over 2021--23, the calibrated score has a
correlation of $0.53$ with the share who worked from home in the reference week and an
occupation-size-weighted slope of $0.40$ (SE $0.09$), so it is relevant. The raw external index
fits better in levels (correlation $0.74$), because swapping ten occupations onto realized shares
while leaving thirty on task-based probabilities mixes two scales. The calibration's contribution
is not a better cross-sectional fit but the removal of ten grossly mis-scored occupations from the
treated tail, and Section~\ref{sec:res-robust} tests whether that is what drives the result. The
forty scores are listed in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:exposure-scores} and plotted in
Figure~\ref{fig:first-stage}.

\emph{Realized.} The realized Israeli WFH share by occupation, anchored to 2021 (no 2020 extract
exists) with a minimum of 200 observations per occupation. Post-treatment by construction, it is
reported as a bound.

A fourth measure serves three roles. For the employment DDD, occupation is undefined for the
non-employed, so that specification uses a pre-period (2017--2019) shift-share index by
demographic cell (sex, age group, education, district, marital status, religion and continent of
birth; 8,884 cells), equal to the mean of the calibrated occupation score over the cell's
pre-period occupational composition and defined for employed and non-employed women alike. The
same index stratifies the selection correction of Section~\ref{sec:lee}, and because a woman's
post-2021 occupation cannot move it, it also provides the bound on occupational sorting in
Section~\ref{sec:res-ddd-diag}. Its dispersion is a third of the occupation-level index's, so
coefficients on the two are compared per standard deviation, never per unit.

\subsection{The raw hours gap}\label{sec:desc-hours}

Mothers work $38.54$ hours per week before 2021 and $38.84$ after, a gain of $0.31$ hours;
childless women move from $40.04$ to $40.08$. The difference between those two changes,
$0.27$ hours, is the difference-in-differences of equation~\eqref{eq:did} without a single
control, and the regression DiD of Table~\ref{tab:hours}, $0.228$ (SE $0.181$), is within four
hundredths of an hour of it. The controls neither manufacture nor conceal an average effect; its
near-absence is already in the arithmetic of four averages. Mothers remain well below childless
women in both periods.

\subsection{Hours year by year}\label{sec:desc-pretrend}

Figure~\ref{fig:hours-by-year} unpools the pre-period. The raw mother-minus-non-mother gap is
$-1.37$ hours in 2017, $-1.48$ in 2018 and $-1.67$ in 2019, three years within a third of an
hour of each other and each within the others' confidence bounds: the groups were not diverging
before treatment. The post-period path is $-1.37$, $-1.53$ and $-0.80$, not monotone and not
individually distinguishable, so the timing reading of Section~\ref{sec:discussion} rests on the
event studies of Section~\ref{sec:results}, not on this series.

\subsection{Exposure and the commuting channel}\label{sec:desc-exposure}

Commuting patterns are consistent with this channel. Mothers were the \emph{more} mobile group in
every year, working outside their locality of residence $48.2\%$ of the time in 2019 against

time constraint for women who were already commuting rather than drawing a home-bound group into
work; it is descriptive evidence, and no specification uses locality of work as an outcome.

%======================================================================
\section{Empirical Strategy}\label{sec:strategy}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Hypothesis}

The flexibility framework of \citet{goldin2014} yields one testable hypothesis. \textbf{H1:}
after 2021, mothers' usual hours conditional on working rise relative to childless women's in
proportion to their occupation's WFH exposure, with no response where the work cannot be done
from home. The exposure gradient, not the average change, is the object of interest.

\subsection{Difference-in-differences}

For outcome $Y_{it}$ (weekly hours conditional on working that week, or employment) we estimate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:did}
Y_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \Mother_i + \beta_2 \Post_t + \beta_3 (\Mother_i \times \Post_t)
+ X'_{it}\gamma + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
where $X_{it}$ is the control vector described above and $\beta_3$ is the differential change in
the outcome for mothers relative to childless women after the shift to remote work.

\subsection{Triple differences}

To test whether the narrowing operates through remote work, we interact the DiD with occupational
WFH exposure:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ddd}
\begin{split}
Y_{it} = {}& \alpha + \delta_1 \Mother_i + \delta_2 \Post_t + \delta_3 \WFH_{j(i)}
+ \delta_4 (\Mother_i \times \Post_t) \\
& + \delta_5 (\Mother_i \times \WFH_{j(i)}) + \delta_6 (\Post_t \times \WFH_{j(i)}) \\
& + \delta_7 (\Mother_i \times \Post_t \times \WFH_{j(i)}) + X'_{it}\gamma + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $j(i)$ is individual $i$'s occupation. $\delta_7$ asks whether the post-shift change for
mothers relative to non-mothers is larger in more exposed occupations. $\delta_6$ absorbs any
post-2021 change in teleworkable occupations common to mothers and childless women, the general
remote-work recovery; $\delta_5$ absorbs the pre-existing level of the penalty across
occupations, so that $\delta_7$ is a change and not a level; and $\delta_4$ is the post-shift
change for mothers at \emph{zero} exposure, not an average effect. Following \citet{olden2022},
the DDD requires a single parallel-trends assumption: that the mother/non-mother gap in high-
versus low-exposure jobs would have evolved in parallel absent the shift to remote work.

For the hours DDD, $\WFH_j$ is the calibrated occupation-level score and the sample is employed
women matched to a two-digit occupation; for the employment DDD, $\WFH$ is the cell-based index,
the sample is all women, and a $\Mother \times$ age-group interaction is added for the pre-period
age imbalance.

Equation~\eqref{eq:ddd} makes each lower-order occupation-level difference linear in exposure,
an unnecessary restriction with forty occupations, so we estimate two relaxations alongside it.
The \emph{saturated} specification replaces every lower-order term with fixed effects,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ddd-sat}
Y_{it} = \delta_7 (\Mother_i \times \Post_t \times \WFH_{j(i)}) + X'_{it}\gamma
+ \lambda_{j(i),t} + \mu_{j(i),\Mother_i} + \nu_{\Mother_i,t} + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{jt}$, $\mu_{j,m}$ and $\nu_{m,t}$ are occupation-by-year, occupation-by-mother
and mother-by-year effects; they absorb, respectively, $\delta_2$, $\delta_3$, $\delta_6$ and
any occupation-specific year shock; $\delta_1$ and $\delta_5$; and $\delta_4$. The \emph{binned}
specification replaces $\WFH_{j(i)}$ with indicators for the
pre-period exposure quartile the occupation falls in, the bins of Figure~\ref{fig:dose-response},
so that the top-versus-bottom contrast carries a standard error. For each DDD we also report the
minimum detectable effect on the reference distribution the model's inference uses,
$\mathrm{MDE} = \mathrm{SE}\,(t_{0.975} + t_{0.80})$, which is $2.87 \times \mathrm{SE}$ with 39
degrees of freedom and $2.80 \times \mathrm{SE}$ under the normal, so that a null can be read as
``genuinely null'' or ``underpowered.''

\subsection{Inference}\label{sec:inference}

The survey is a rotating panel. The $258{,}175$ reference-week workers of Table~\ref{tab:descriptives}
come from $64{,}602$ distinct respondents, about four observations each and two to three within
a single survey year, with $96\%$ of rows belonging to someone who appears more than once; the
regressions use $251{,}857$ of those rows from $63{,}199$ respondents, the difference being the
$6{,}318$ rows whose education code is missing (education is the only control with missing
values). Treating the observations as independent would understate standard errors by a factor
of roughly $1.8$, so equation~\eqref{eq:did} and every descriptive interval in
Section~\ref{sec:descriptives} are clustered by individual. What the panel structure does
\emph{not} support is within-person identification: of the $80{,}560$ women in the full sample,
$1.9\%$ are interviewed on both sides of the pre/post boundary, so an individual fixed-effects
design was considered and rejected.
% Provenance: 1,514 of 80,560 distinct individuals (1.88%) appear in both the pre- and post-period
% (check_idpuf_panel_structure(), scripts/validation.R). The 6,318 rows with a missing education
% code (TeudaGvoha 99 -> NA) are the only listwise deletions: confirmed on cleaned_df.rds,
% 2026-09-23, with zero NAs in the other four controls on the hours sample.

For the hours DDD the regressor is constant within a two-digit occupation, and standard errors are
clustered at that level, leaving forty clusters. The reported $p$-values use a $t$ reference
distribution with $G - 1 = 39$ degrees of freedom and a cluster-count adjustment to the variance.
Forty is few enough that we do not rest on that approximation: for the headline coefficient and
for the pre-period coefficients of the triple-difference event study we also report wild cluster
bootstrap $p$-values, with Rademacher weights, the null imposed and $9{,}999$ draws
\citep{roodman2019, fischer2021}, and Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics} adds a permutation test that
relies on no asymptotic approximation at all. The joint pre-period test remains the analytic Wald
$F$, since the joint bootstrap is not available in that implementation without a Julia toolchain.
The employment DDD is clustered on the age-by-education-by-district cell (about 210 clusters).

\subsection{Selection into employment: Lee bounds}\label{sec:lee}

Hours are observed only for women who are working, and whether a woman is working is itself an
outcome of the same treatment: if remote work pulls marginal mothers into work after 2021, the
post-period sample of working mothers differs from the pre-period one for reasons unrelated to
hours choices. We address this with trimming bounds in the spirit of \citet{lee2009}, adapted to
the two-by-two design. Let $s_{ab} = \Pr(\text{hours observed} \mid \Mother=a, \Post=b)$, the
probability of being employed \emph{and} working in the reference week. Under parallel trends in
selection the counterfactual for post-period mothers is $s^*_{11} = s_{10} + (s_{01} - s_{00})$;
if the observed $s_{11}$ exceeds it, the excess share $1 - s^*_{11}/s_{11}$ is trimmed from the
$(\Mother=1,\Post=1)$ cell's hours distribution, from the top for a lower bound and from the
bottom for an upper bound, and equation~\eqref{eq:did} is re-estimated on each trimmed sample.
Trimming from a single tail assumes monotone selection. For the DDD the counterfactual is
computed within each quartile of the cell-based exposure index, which unlike the occupation-level
index is observable for the non-employed; each quartile's post-period mothers are trimmed by
their own excess share, the trimmed subsets are recombined, and equation~\eqref{eq:ddd} is refit
with the occupation-level regressor. This assumes selection depends on exposure only through the
cell-index quartile. Because the parameter is partially identified we also report the
\citet{imbens2004} confidence interval for the identified set. The construction handles only
\emph{excess} selection in the treated cell (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Diagnostics, falsification, and the fathers' comparison}\label{sec:diagnostics}

We estimate event-study versions of equation~\eqref{eq:did} with $\Mother \times$ year
interactions (reference year 2019) for both outcomes and test the pre-period coefficients jointly.
Because those test the difference-in-differences assumption rather than the triple-differences
one, we also estimate the event-study version of equation~\eqref{eq:ddd} itself, with $\Post$
replaced by survey-year interactions at every level, and apply the same joint test to its
pre-period coefficients.

The falsification test is a permutation test on the exposure score. Under the sharp null that
teleworkability is unrelated to the post-2021 change in the motherhood gap, the forty occupation
scores are exchangeable across occupations, so we reassign them at random across the forty
occupation codes $999$ times, holding every woman's occupation and the mother/period structure
fixed, refit equation~\eqref{eq:ddd}, and record the cluster-robust $t$-statistic of the triple
interaction. The permutation $p$-value is the share of draws, counting the observed one, whose
$|t|$ reaches the observed value \citep{phipson2010}. It needs neither the $t(39)$ approximation
nor a bootstrap, and it answers the question a placebo should: how often would a design like this
one produce an exposure gradient as large as ours when exposure is, by construction, noise.

Occupation is observed when the outcome is, so it is itself a potential outcome: if remote work
drew mothers into teleworkable occupations after 2021, the post-period composition of mothers
inside high-exposure occupations changes for reasons unrelated to hours. We check this directly
with a difference-in-differences that takes the exposure score itself as the outcome, and bound
it by re-estimating the hours DDD on the pre-period cell index of Section~\ref{sec:exposure},
which a woman's later occupation cannot move.

The remaining checks re-estimate the hours DDD with an added $\Mother \times$ age-group
interaction and with pre-period age-group raking weights; with the external and realized indices
in place of the calibrated one, with the index calibrated on men only, and with the ten swapped
occupations given their own $\Mother \times \Post$ terms; with each occupation removed in turn;
with alternative codings of the outcome; separately for Jewish and Arab women; by the age of the
youngest child; and on the male subsample with ``mother'' read as ``father.'' The last is a
comparison, not a placebo: fathers in teleworkable occupations are exposed to remote work exactly
as mothers are, so a negative gradient would be evidence on household reallocation rather than on
a confound. Coefficients across disjoint subsamples are compared with two-sample $z$-tests.

\subsection{The hours DDD: the paper's headline result}\label{sec:res-ddd}

Column~(2) of Table~\ref{tab:hours} interacts the DiD with the calibrated occupation-level
exposure index. The triple interaction is $3.224$\sym{**} (SE $1.022$, $N = 246{,}326$): among
working mothers, those in more WFH-exposed occupations saw a larger post-2021 increase in weekly
hours relative to childless women in the same occupations. The index has a standard deviation of
$0.199$, so the estimate is $0.64$ hours per standard deviation, and between the quartiles of
Figure~\ref{fig:dose-response}, whose mean exposures are $0.06$ and $0.59$, the regression
implies $1.71$ hours a week against $1.87$ from the raw cell means. At zero exposure the implied
change for mothers is $-0.539$ (SE $0.336$), and because $\Post \times \WFH$ is in the model the
gradient is net of whatever happened to hours in teleworkable occupations for all women: outside
them there is no gain to speak of. The estimate is $3.15$ analytic standard errors from zero,
qualified by the bootstrap and permutation $p$-values of Section~\ref{sec:res-robust}; the
minimum detectable effect on the $t_{39}$ reference is $2.94$ hours per unit, or $0.58$ per
standard deviation, so an effect of this size is detectable without much room to spare.

Columns~(3) and~(4) relax the functional form. With occupation-by-year, occupation-by-mother and
mother-by-year fixed effects in place of every lower-order term (equation~\eqref{eq:ddd-sat}),
the triple interaction is $2.876$ (SE $0.950$), a tenth below the pooled estimate and still
significant at the $1\%$ level on the analytic standard error. Column~(4) replaces the score with
indicators for the pre-period exposure quartile: relative to the least teleworkable quartile, the
post-2021 change in the gap is $0.753$ hours (SE $0.483$) in the second quartile, $0.600$
(SE $0.485$) in the third and $1.844$ (SE $0.595$, $p < 0.01$) in the top quartile, the only
contrast that is individually significant. The response is a step at the top of the exposure
distribution more than a smooth gradient, and that top quartile holds six of the forty
occupations, which is why it matters that the pooled and saturated columns, which use all forty,
agree with it in sign and magnitude, and that no single occupation drives it
(Section~\ref{sec:res-robust}).

\paragraph{Pre-trends.}
The pre-trend test of Figure~\ref{fig:pretrend} licenses equation~\eqref{eq:did}, not

\subsection{Robustness}\label{sec:res-robust}

Table~\ref{tab:robust} collects the remaining checks.

\paragraph{Exposure measure.}
With the raw \citet{dingel2020} external index in place of the calibrated one the triple
interaction is $0.672$ (SE $0.884$), positive but insignificant; with the realized 2021-anchored
index it is $5.195$\sym{*} (SE $2.363$). The external index is the only one built entirely from
pre-treatment information and it yields the weakest estimate, so the natural objection is that
calibrating on 2022--23 realized WFH lets the regressor absorb post-period outcomes. Three checks
address it while holding the sample at all forty occupations. Calibrating on men only, so that no
woman in the regression contributes to her own regressor, swaps twelve occupations rather than
ten and gives $3.869$\sym{***} (SE $0.979$). Keeping the external score as the regressor but
letting the ten swapped occupations carry their own $\Mother \times \Post$ change gives an
external-score gradient of $2.339$ (SE $1.352$, $p = 0.09$) beside a negative change for the
swapped group ($-1.347$, SE $0.926$): the occupations the external index places in the treated
tail did not respond, and once they are allowed not to, the gradient in the external score
re-emerges at two thirds of the headline. Dropping them altogether, so that the two indices
coincide on the thirty occupations that remain, gives $4.309$\sym{**} (SE $1.192$, 30 clusters).
The calibration does not produce the result; what it does is remove from the treated tail
occupations, teaching and clerical work above all, that returned to the workplace whatever their
task content. What it cannot be made into is a pre-treatment measure, and a reader who weights
the external index above the others will read the headline as directionally supported rather
than established.

\paragraph{Covariate balance and age.}
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:balance-quartile} reports the pre-period difference between mothers and
childless women in every control within each exposure quartile of the estimation sample, and
the differences vary
with exposure: mothers are younger than childless women by $0.54$ of an age-group code in the
least teleworkable quartile and older by $0.08$ in the most, the academic-degree gap falls from
$10.8$ percentage points to under $2$, and the marriage gap rises from $32$ to $43$ points. A
gap that itself varies with exposure is what the triple interaction is identified off, so two
specifications relax the age control. A $\Mother \times$ age-group interaction leaves the
estimate essentially unchanged ($3.205$\sym{**}, SE $1.055$); reweighting to balance pre-period
age groups attenuates it by about 19\% to $2.597$\sym{*} (SE $1.050$), still significant at the
5\% level but below the design's minimum detectable effect, the largest single move any check
produces.

\paragraph{Sample.}
Excluding the 2023 survey year, whose final quarter includes the start of the October 2023 war
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), the triple interaction is $2.979$\sym{*} (SE $1.170$), smaller
because 2023 carries the largest event-study coefficient but still significant at the 5\% level,
while the plain hours DiD stays a null ($0.071$, SE $0.198$). Removing each of the forty
occupations in turn (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:loo}) leaves the estimate between $2.415$, without
science and engineering professionals, and $3.580$, without business and administration
associate professionals; the largest move is $0.79$ of the headline standard error, no refit
leaves the band of one standard error around the headline, and every refit is significant at the
5\% level on its own analytic standard error ($p \leq 0.017$). Neither the six top-quartile
occupations nor the largest cells carry the result on their own.

\paragraph{Outcome coding.}
The hours variable is a bin-median approximation and the effect is smaller than one bin width,
so the result should not depend on how the bins are turned into hours. Dropping the two imputed
irregular-hours codes gives $3.056$\sym{**} (SE $1.059$). Because 35 and 40 hours are exact bin
edges, the same specification can be estimated as a linear probability model: the probability of
usually working at least 35 hours rises by $0.096$\sym{**} (SE $0.029$) per unit of exposure for
mothers relative to childless women after 2021, and of at least 40 hours by $0.107$\sym{**}
(SE $0.038$), or $1.9$ and $2.1$ percentage points per standard deviation against pre-period
shares of $71\%$ and $61\%$. The effect is a shift of mothers in teleworkable jobs across the
full-time boundary.

\paragraph{Inference.}
Clustering two ways, by individual and by occupation, leaves the headline standard error
unchanged at $1.022$. The two procedures that address the forty-cluster problem disagree with
the analytic $p$-value of $0.003$: the wild cluster bootstrap gives $p = 0.056$, with a bootstrap
95\% confidence set of $[-0.26, 5.84]$, and the permutation test gives $p = 0.032$, with $31$ of
$999$ reassignments of the forty scores producing a $|t|$ at least as large as the observed
$3.15$. The estimate is significant at the $1\%$, $5\%$ and $10\%$ levels under the three
procedures, and we report all three rather than choosing among them; the bootstrap, which
re-signs whole-cluster residuals, is the most conservative, while the permutation test holds
the cluster structure fixed and randomizes only the regressor, the sharper question for a design
whose identifying variation is occupation-level exposure.

\subsection{Falsification: permuting exposure across occupations}\label{sec:res-falsification}

Figure~\ref{fig:permutation} plots the reference distribution the permutation test builds. Each
of the $999$ draws reassigns the forty calibrated scores at random across the forty occupation
codes, leaving every woman's occupation, mother status and survey year untouched, and refits
equation~\eqref{eq:ddd}. Under this sharp null the central $95\%$ of cluster-robust
$t$-statistics lie between $-2.96$ and $2.60$; the observed $3.15$ is exceeded in absolute value
by $31$ draws, so $p = 0.032$ (Monte-Carlo standard error $0.006$). A design with this cluster
structure produces an exposure gradient as large as ours about three times in a hundred when
exposure carries no information, which is the falsification a placebo should supply and a
fathers' regression, a different and also-treated population, cannot.

\subsection{Heterogeneity by the age of the youngest child}\label{sec:res-childage}

If remote work relaxes a time constraint that binds through child care, the response should be
largest for mothers of young children and smallest for mothers of teenagers.
Table~\ref{tab:childage} and Figure~\ref{fig:childage} re-estimate the hours DiD and DDD four

\subsection{Heterogeneity by population group, and the fathers' comparison}\label{sec:res-subgroup}

Table~\ref{tab:subgroup} re-estimates the hours DiD and DDD for Jewish and Arab women and for
men. For Jewish women the pattern is the pooled pattern: a DiD of $0.302$ (SE $0.203$) and a DDD
of $2.847$\sym{**} (SE $0.913$). For Arab women the DiD is $-0.072$ (SE $0.479$) and the DDD
$5.507$\sym{*} (SE $2.674$), nominally twice the Jewish estimate but statistically
indistinguishable from it ($z = 0.942$, $p = 0.35$) and resting on a subsample nine times smaller
and concentrated in few occupations; it is imprecise rather than a documented heterogeneous
effect.

The fathers' comparison is a comparison, not a placebo. Re-estimating the same models on men,
with ``mother'' read as ``father,'' gives a DiD of $-0.400$\sym{*} (SE $0.188$) and a DDD of
$-1.762$ (SE $1.015$, $p = 0.09$). Fathers in teleworkable occupations are exposed to remote work
exactly as mothers are, so a null was never the prediction; what the comparison shows is that
the exposure gradient has the opposite sign for fathers, and that the two gradients are
distinguishable ($z = 3.461$, $p = 0.0005$). A macro trend common to all workers in teleworkable
jobs cannot produce a positive gradient for mothers beside a negative one for fathers, and a
negative gradient for fathers is what within-household reallocation would look like if mothers'
additional paid hours were matched by fathers' additional care hours. We report that as a reading
the data are consistent with, not as a result the design identifies.

\subsection{The extensive margin, briefly}\label{sec:res-extensive}

Column~(1) of Table~\ref{tab:extensive} reports equation~\eqref{eq:did} with employment as the
outcome: $\Mother \times \Post = -0.0053$ (SE $0.0052$). The mother/non-mother employment gap did
not change after the shift to remote work, and with a standard error of half a percentage point
this is an informative null; the pre-period event-study coefficients pass their joint test
($F(2, 79{,}069) = 0.63$, $p = 0.53$). Column~(2) reports the employment DDD with the cell-based
index: $-0.0257$ (SE $0.0725$). This null is of a different kind. The design could detect a triple
interaction no smaller than $1.43$ percentage points per standard deviation of the cell index, or
$1.85\%$ of the baseline employment rate, and against \citeauthor{harrington2025}'s $0.78$
percentage points per 10\% rise in WFH it is underpowered by a factor of two to three. The
shortfall is structural, a binary outcome and a regressor with a standard deviation of $0.07$,
and re-clustering, combining the two margins and refining the cell partition did not close it.
The employment DDD is therefore \emph{uninformative}, evidence neither for nor against a
WFH-mediated employment effect, and the two nulls in Table~\ref{tab:extensive} should not be
read as one verdict. This is what motivates the intensive-margin design, where occupation-level
exposure is available.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
%======================================================================

Among mothers who remain in work there is no post-2021 hours effect on average, and a large one
in teleworkable occupations. The conjunction is the paper's result: a triple interaction of about
$3.2$ hours per unit of exposure, an implied change at zero exposure that is if anything
negative, and a selection correction the estimate survives. This is consistent with WFH access
relaxing a binding time constraint for mothers in teleworkable jobs, the \citet{goldin2014}
mechanism, and hard to reconcile with a general recovery common to all women, which would have
raised hours across occupations rather than only where the work can be done from home.

How precisely the effect is estimated is a separate question from whether it is there. The
three inference procedures of Section~\ref{sec:res-robust} place the estimate at the $1\%$, $5\%$
and $10\%$ levels, and what makes the finding credible is the pattern of evidence rather than any
one $p$-value: a saturated specification that changes nothing, a top-quartile contrast
significant on its own, a leave-one-out range that never leaves the headline's standard error, a
monotone child-age gradient, an opposite-signed fathers' gradient and a selection correction that
barely moves the bounds. Which occupations do the work is concrete: the calibration moved teaching
and clerical support sharply down, because Israeli practice kept them in person regardless of
task content, while ICT professionals stayed high on both measures, and the gradient is
identified off that contrast. The checks that calibrate on men only or hold the sample fixed
while letting the swapped occupations differ show the calibration is not what produces the
result.

The magnitude is economically meaningful where it applies. For a mother at the top quartile's
mean exposure the regression implies a post-2021 change of about $1.4$ hours a week
($-0.54 + 3.22 \times 0.59$), and the raw top-quartile difference-in-differences of
Figure~\ref{fig:dose-response} is $1.55$. The penalty it is set against must be the penalty in
those same jobs, not the average one: column~(2) of Table~\ref{tab:hours} puts the pre-period
motherhood gap at $-1.45 - 1.30 \times 0.59 \approx -2.2$ hours at that exposure, so mothers in
the most teleworkable jobs recovered roughly three fifths of their penalty after 2021. At the
bottom quartile's mean exposure of $0.06$ the implied change is $-0.35$ hours: mothers in the
least teleworkable jobs recovered none of it. The size is what the most literal form of the
time-constraint mechanism would produce: \citet{aksoy2023} put the commuting time saved at about
72 minutes per day worked from home, so two remote days a week free roughly two and a half hours,
and a mother who returns most of that time to paid work is a mother whose hours rise by about the
amount estimated here.

A general recovery is not the only alternative to that mechanism; three demand-side accounts
also fit a gradient by teleworkability. Employers in professional and ICT occupations may have
changed the contracts they offer mothers after 2021, with no change in mothers' constraints; the
saturated specification absorbs any occupation-by-year shock common to mothers and childless
women, so this needs a change aimed at mothers in teleworkable occupations specifically. The
2021--22 hiring boom in the technology sector raised hours in exposed occupations for everyone;
the fathers' opposite-signed gradient rules out a shock common to all workers in those
occupations, but not one that fell on mothers alone. And usual hours are self-reported: if
mothers working from home report the hours they are available while fathers report the hours
they work, the two gradients would diverge as observed with no change in labor supplied; the
child-age gradient weighs against this, since a reporting difference has no reason to be twice as
large for mothers of infants as for mothers of teenagers while a binding time constraint does.
None of the three is excluded by the design, and we read the exposure gradient as the
time-constraint mechanism's signature rather than its proof.

The timing is also informative. The DDD event study rises from $3.0$ in 2021 to $4.0$ in 2023,
consistent with WFH adoption and household time reallocation being a multi-year adjustment; the
2023 coefficient is the largest on both margins and, for the reason given in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}, should be held more loosely than the others.

Why hours and not employment? The employment DDD is not a failed test of the mechanism but an
uninformative one, underpowered by a factor of two to three against the closest published effect.
Substantively the two results are coherent: the effect operates on \emph{how much} mothers in
teleworkable jobs work, not on \emph{whether} they are employed, which is the margin
\citet{kleven2019denmark} identify as a leading channel of the child penalty. This is where our
findings depart from \citet{harrington2025}, whose US evidence is on employment and income. The
policy reading is correspondingly narrow: WFH availability matters for how intensively mothers
who already hold \emph{teleworkable} jobs can work, not for whether mothers in general hold jobs.
The one heterogeneity we read substantively is by the age of the youngest child, because the
mechanism predicts its direction in advance; the Arab-women estimate is larger than the Jewish
one but imprecise and statistically indistinguishable from it, and we do not claim heterogeneity
on its basis.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{Identification.}
The triple difference's own pre-trend test (Section~\ref{sec:res-ddd-diag}) shows no visible
violation but lacks the power to rule one out; the exposure measure that delivers the headline
draws on post-period Israeli data for its calibration, and although the checks of
Section~\ref{sec:res-robust} show the calibration does not produce the result, the fully
pre-treatment external index yields a positive and insignificant estimate; the crosswalk behind
that external index is undocumented (Section~\ref{sec:exposure}); and the sorting check finds no
post-2021 movement of mothers across exposure levels but bounds the effect only imprecisely on a
regressor fixed before treatment. The three inference procedures place the headline at the
$1\%$, $5\%$ and $10\%$ levels, and that range, not the analytic $p$-value, is the paper's
statement of how precisely the effect is estimated.

\paragraph{The narrowed hours estimand.}
Hours are usual hours among women who were working, not among all women holding a job, and
roughly $10\%$ of each year's employed sample is excluded on that basis. The exclusion is not
innocuous for the triple interaction because absence is related to exposure: it runs at $14.4\%$
of the employed in the least teleworkable quartile against $8.5$--$9.6\%$ in the other three, and
among mothers it fell after 2021 in the top quartile while rising in the bottom
one.\footnote{Reference-week absence among employed women with a matched occupation, by the
quartiles of Figure~\ref{fig:dose-response}: $14.4\%$, $8.5\%$, $9.5\%$ and $9.6\%$. Among mothers
the top-quartile share fell from $12.1\%$ to $10.4\%$ after 2021 and the bottom-quartile share
rose from $15.5\%$ to $16.9\%$; among childless women the top-quartile share is $6.1\%$ in both
periods.} Movement across the absence margin is part of what selection into the observed-hours
sample means here, which is why the bounds of Section~\ref{sec:lee} define selection on that
sample. They handle only \emph{excess} selection in the post-period mother cell, assume monotone
selection to justify trimming from a single tail, and found little to correct; that is not the
same as the concern being absent.

\paragraph{Survey weights.}
CBS survey weights are intentionally not applied in any outcome regression; reported rates and
coefficients are unweighted estimates on the analysis sample, not population-representative
statistics. The one exception is internal to constructing the cell-based exposure index.

\paragraph{The 2023 survey year.}
The 2023 coefficient is the largest in both event studies, and the 2023 survey year includes the
quarter in which the war that began on 7 October 2023 disrupted the labor market: reserve
call-ups, which bear on the fathers' comparison, and school closures and evacuations, which bear
on mothers' hours. The estimate survives dropping the year and 2021 and 2022 are individually
significant on the triple difference, but the 2023 point should be held more loosely. Early 2021
likewise included a third lockdown, so the post-period is ``post-acute'' more cleanly in its
later years than in its first.
% The exclusion is of the whole year: the survey-month field (ChodeshSeker) is dropped at cleaning,
% so a fourth-quarter-only exclusion would need a schema change (audit item I5).

\paragraph{Who is childless, and age.}
The control group is any woman aged 25--59 with no child under 17 in the household, so it
includes women whose children are grown and women who become mothers within the window, as in
\citet{harrington2025}; if part of it responds to the treatment as mothers do, the DiD is biased
toward zero. Age enters as a categorical code because the extract has no continuous age, and
mothers and non-mothers are imbalanced on it in a way that tracks exposure
(Table~\ref{tab:balance-quartile}); the age-interacted and reweighted specifications address
this and the estimate survives, but the reweighted attenuation ($3.224 \to 2.597$) is the largest
single move any check produces.

\paragraph{Exposure is measured coarsely, and on a mixed scale.}
Exposure is assigned at the two-digit occupation level, forty groups, and \citet{buzaglo2023}
shows that within-firm sorting matters as much as occupation for Israeli gender gaps. The
calibrated score mixes task-based probabilities for thirty occupations with realized shares for
ten, which costs cross-sectional fit; a score on a single scale is the realized index of
Table~\ref{tab:robust}, which gives a larger and noisier estimate. Within-occupation
heterogeneity in teleworkability is classical measurement error in the regressor and attenuates
the triple interaction toward zero. Occupation codes masked by the CBS for disclosure control are
dropped when building exposure; a proxy check finds no strong evidence of bias from this. The
panel does not support within-person identification: $1.9\%$ of women span the pre/post
boundary.
