# Intensive-Margin (Hours) Analysis: Core Narrative

**Scope note**: This document covers the intensive margin (hours worked) only, per an explicit
scoping decision for this analysis. The extensive-margin (employment) DiD/DDD results
(`outputs/basic_reg_table.csv`, `outputs/ddd_employment.csv`) exist in the pipeline and are
mentioned only where needed for context (§4) — they are not analyzed here. All results below are
generated by `main.R` against the real CBS extract; every number cites its source file in
`outputs/` so it can be checked directly against the repo's own artifacts.

## 1. Data Validation & Diagnostics

**Missingness.** `WorkHoursCont` is missing for 22.6% of the analysis sample
(`outputs/comparative_stats_missing_pct.csv`). This is mechanical, not a data-quality problem: the
variable is defined only for `Employed == 1` (`scripts/data_processing.R`), and 22.6% is
essentially the sample's non-employment rate. No separate missing-data correction is needed for
`WorkHoursCont` itself; the sample-selection concern it does create (conditioning hours on
employment) is handled directly by the Lee-bounds analysis in §3, not by imputation.

**Event-study pre-trend** (`outputs/hours_diagnostics_pretrend_table.csv`,
`outputs/event_study_pretrend_hours.pdf`). Coefficients are `Mother x ShnatSeker`, reference year
2019:

| Year | Coefficient | SE | Significant? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2017 | **-1.524** | 0.277 | *** |
| 2018 | 0.122 | 0.214 | no |
| 2021 | 0.307 | 0.257 | no |
| 2022 | 0.108 | 0.262 | no |
| 2023 | **0.744** | 0.261 | ** |

This is a genuine, statistically significant **pre-trend violation in 2017**: the plotted 95% CI
(`[-2.06, -0.98]`) sits entirely below zero and does not overlap 2018 or 2019. A formal joint Wald
test on the two pre-period coefficients (2017, 2018) confirms this is not just one noisy point:
**F(2, 65,088) = 23.7, p = 5.2e-11** (console output, `run_pretrend_joint_test()` on
`hours_diagnostics_results$pretrend_model`) — strongly rejects the joint null that mothers' hours
were trending the same as non-mothers' hours across the full pre-period. By contrast, 2018 alone is
flat and insignificant relative to 2019, so the *shorter* 2018-2019 window is consistent with
parallel trends.

**Implication for identification**: the paper's parallel-trends assumption should be stated as
resting on the 2018-2019 comparison, not the full 2017-2019 window, and the 2017 anomaly should be
disclosed as a limitation rather than smoothed over. (For contrast: the same joint Wald test on the
*employment* outcome passes cleanly, F(2, 79,069) = 0.63, p = 0.53 — the pre-trend problem is
specific to the hours outcome, not a general data issue.)

**Post-period shape.** The effect is not an immediate 2021 jump — it rises gradually, only becoming
significant by 2023 (0.744**). This pattern is itself substantively interesting and is picked up
again in §4.

**Balance tests.** The one balance check run against real data is the `GilNK` (age-group) imbalance
diagnostic (`docs/decisions/age-balance-robustness-chain.md`,
`outputs/age_balance_robustness_age_imbalance_by_quartile.csv`): the pre-period age gap between
mothers and non-mothers is **-0.797 GilNK units at the lowest WFH-exposure quartile, shrinking
monotonically to +0.239 at the highest quartile**. This gap tracks `WFH_Exposure` quartile — exactly
the hours DDD's own regressor — so an additive age control cannot fully absorb it. This is addressed
directly in §3 (age-interacted and reweighted specs, now run against real data for the hours DDD
specifically).

## 2. Main Specifications

**Primary DiD** (`outputs/intensive_margin_table.csv`, N=281,750, clustered by IDPUF):

> `Mother x Post = 0.826*** (SE 0.183)`

Mothers' weekly hours rose ~0.83 hours more than non-mothers' post-2021, on average.

**Primary DDD** (`outputs/ddd_hours_table.csv`, calibrated occupation-level exposure, N=275,708,
clustered by occupation):

> `Mother:Post:WFH_Exposure = 3.407*** (SE 0.930)`

This is the paper's headline result. Note that once the exposure interactions enter the model, the
bare `Mother x Post` term collapses to **0.015 (ns)** — the average hours effect found in the plain
DiD is not a broad, occupation-independent shift; it is fully absorbed into exposure heterogeneity.
The DDD, not the plain DiD, should be presented as the substantive finding; the plain DiD's 0.826
should be framed as suggestive but fragile (confirmed in §3).

**Mechanism regression** (per-occupation `Mother:Post` point estimates regressed on occupation
`wfh_exposure`, precision-weighted by `1/se_j^2`; console output from `run_hours_ddd_regression()`,
underlying data in `outputs/ddd_calibrated_mechanism_data.csv`):

> slope = **2.639 (SE 0.899), p = 0.006**, R² = 0.197, n = 37 occupations (3 of 40 dropped for
> degenerate per-occupation fits — `outputs/ddd_calibrated_dropped_occupations_data.csv`)

This is a second, independent piece of evidence for the same relationship, estimated at the
occupation level rather than pooled across individuals — the individual-level triple interaction and
the occupation-level mechanism slope tell the same story from two different angles.

## 3. Robustness Checks

**Lee (2009) bounds, plain DiD** (`outputs/intensive_margin_lee_bounds_table.csv`): point 0.826,
bounds `[0.213, 1.313]`, **95% Imbens-Manski CI = [-0.084, 1.612]** — crosses zero. The plain hours
DiD does **not** survive a selection-on-employment correction. This is the fragility flagged in §2.

**Lee bounds, DDD** (`outputs/hours_lee_bounds_table.csv`, `outputs/hours_lee_bounds_quartiles.csv`):
excess selection is modest in all four `WFH_Exposure` quartiles (trim proportions 0.25%-4.6%, largest
in Q1, the least-teleworkable occupations). Bounds stay tight and positive:

| Bound | Coef | SE | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lower | 3.441 | 0.993 | [1.496, 5.387] |
| Point | 3.408 | 0.956 | [1.533, 5.282] |
| Upper | 3.457 | 0.897 | [1.698, 5.216] |

**95% Imbens-Manski CI for the identified set = [1.50, 5.21]** — excludes zero throughout. Unlike the
plain DiD, the triple interaction survives the selection correction. This is the strongest single
piece of evidence in the paper: the result is not an artifact of differential selection into
employment.

**Minimum detectable effect**: 2.606 (68% of the 3.41 point estimate) — the design is well-powered
for an effect of this size (`compute_ddd_mde()` console output).

**Exposure-measure sensitivity** (`outputs/ddd_hours_external.csv`, `outputs/ddd_hours_realized.csv`):

| Exposure measure | Mother:Post:WFH_Exposure | SE | Sig. |
|---|---|---|---|
| External (Dingel-Neiman) | 0.940 | 0.865 | ns |
| Calibrated (primary) | 3.407 | 0.930 | *** |
| Realized (2021 anchor) | 6.416 | 2.454 | * |

Direction is consistent across all three; magnitude and significance are not. The calibrated measure
is the one purpose-built to correct known external-index misclassification — e.g. teaching (ISCO 23)
scores 0.966 on the external index but only 0.069 realized/calibrated
(`outputs/wfh_exposure_calibrated.csv`), because Israeli schools stayed in-person by policy despite
being "teleworkable" by US task content
(`docs/decisions/calibrated-exposure-and-cell-ddd.md`). The paper should lead with the calibrated
estimate and report the other two as sensitivity, framed as "directionally robust, not point-estimate
robust."

**Age-balance robustness — now run against real data.** The hours-DDD analogs of the age-interacted
and reweighted specs (`run_hours_ddd_age_interacted()`, `run_hours_ddd_reweighted()`) had
implemented methodology but had never actually been executed against the real extract or exported
before this analysis (no corresponding CSVs existed in `outputs/`). They were run for this document
(`RUN_AGE_BALANCE_ROBUSTNESS <- TRUE`, one diagnostic rerun of `main.R`, flag reverted to its
committed `FALSE` default afterward) and exported to
`outputs/age_balance_robustness_hours_ddd_age_interacted.csv` and
`..._hours_ddd_reweighted.csv`:

| Spec | Mother:Post:WFH_Exposure | SE | Sig. |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary (no age interaction) | 3.407 | 0.930 | *** |
| Age-interacted (`+ Mother:GilNK`) | 3.367 | 0.955 | ** |
| Reweighted (pre-period `GilNK`-raking) | 2.836 | 0.906 | ** |

The triple interaction **survives both checks**: barely moved by adding `Mother:GilNK` (3.407 →
3.367), and attenuated but still significant at p<0.01 under reweighting (3.407 → 2.836, about a 17%
reduction). The confirmed age imbalance (§1) does not overturn the headline result, though the
reweighted attenuation is worth reporting rather than omitting — it's the largest single move any
robustness check produces on this coefficient.

A secondary finding falls out of the age-interacted spec: `Mother x GilNK4 = -2.083*** (0.487)`,
`Mother x GilNK6 = 1.063* (0.525)`, `Mother x GilNK7 = 1.337* (0.596)` — a significant,
non-monotonic age pattern in the hours penalty itself, independent of WFH exposure. This mirrors a
similar age-heterogeneity finding already documented for the employment DDD
(`docs/decisions/age-balance-robustness-chain.md`) and is worth a sentence in the paper as a
distinct, incidental finding, not a rescue or complication of the main WFH-mechanism result.

**ISCO disclosure-masking sensitivity** (`outputs/isco_masking_sensitivity_by_group.csv`,
`_comparison_wide.csv`): the masked-vs-unmasked realized-WFH gap is small and inconsistently signed
across ISCO1 major groups (+0.134 in group 2 (professionals), -0.086 in group 6, -0.085 in group 5,
etc.), and the pooled regression coefficient on `ISCO_masked` is only marginally significant (0.045*,
SE 0.021) with a negligible within-R² (9.45e-5). This is reassuring but weak evidence — report as "no
strong evidence that disclosure masking biases the exposure measure," not as a clean bill of health.

**Known, un-fixable limitations to state plainly in the paper:**
- Lee bounds here only correct *excess* selection in the `Mother=1,Post=1` cell; under-selection is a
  missing-data problem this construction cannot address
  (`docs/decisions/intensive-margin-lee-bounds.md`).
- The Lee-bounds counterfactual itself relies on the same parallel-trends assumption already dented
  by the 2017 finding in §1.
- Monotone selection is assumed (WFH weakly increases, never decreases, employment probability).
- No CBS survey weights (`MishkalSofi`, etc.) are applied anywhere in this project's outcome
  regressions — a deliberate, project-wide scope decision (`CLAUDE.md`, README "Known limitations"),
  not something specific to this result.

## 4. Economic Interpretation

Among mothers who remain employed, the post-2021 hours effect is **not uniform across occupations**:
it is concentrated in occupations with higher WFH exposure (calibrated measure), with a
triple-interaction coefficient of ~3.4 additional weekly hours per unit of exposure, robust to a Lee-
bounds correction for selection into employment. This is consistent with WFH access relaxing a
binding time constraint specifically for mothers in teleworkable jobs — not a general post-pandemic
labor-market recovery common to all women, since the effect scales with occupational exposure rather
than appearing uniformly.

**Which occupations are doing the work.** The calibration step (`calibrate_isco_exposure()`) swapped
10 of 40 ISCO-2 occupations away from their external Dingel-Neiman score toward realized Israeli
behavior where the gap was large and statistically distinguishable from noise
(`outputs/wfh_exposure_calibrated.csv`). Concretely: clerical support (ISCO 41, calibrated 0.098,
down from external 1.0) and teaching (ISCO 23, calibrated 0.069, down from 0.966) were corrected
sharply downward — both occupations the external index over-predicts as teleworkable relative to
Israeli institutional practice — while occupations like ICT professionals (ISCO 25, calibrated 0.360,
external 1.0) remained high on both measures. This grounds `WFH_Exposure` in concrete, checkable
occupational content rather than leaving it as an abstract regressor.

**Timing.** The gradual rise from an insignificant 2021 estimate to a significant 2023 one (§1) is
consistent with WFH adoption and household time-reallocation being a multi-year adjustment process,
not an instantaneous response to the initial 2020-21 shock — plausible given that occupational WFH
norms themselves were still settling over 2021-2023 (`docs/decisions/checkpoint6-wfh-anchor-year.md`
makes the same point about the exposure measure's own anchor-year choice).

**Why hours, not employment.** This project's primary specification pivoted from the extensive
margin (employment) to the intensive margin (hours) specifically because the employment DDD remained
underpowered even after two rounds of exposure-measure fixes (MDE ~26% of baseline, roughly 4x the
point estimate — `docs/decisions/hours-ddd-pivot.md`). The hours mechanism is well-powered (MDE 68%
of the point estimate, §3) and the underlying economic story is coherent with that pivot: the effect
operates on *how much* mothers who stay employed work, conditional on remaining employed, not on
*whether* they are employed at all. The (out-of-scope) employment DDD's null result is consistent
with this framing rather than contradicting it.
