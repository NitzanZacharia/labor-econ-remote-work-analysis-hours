# Intensive-Margin (Hours) Analysis: Core Narrative

**Scope note**: This document covers the intensive margin (hours worked) only, per an explicit
scoping decision for this analysis. The extensive-margin (employment) DiD/DDD results
(`outputs/basic_reg_table.csv`, `outputs/ddd_employment.csv`) exist in the pipeline and are
mentioned only where needed for context (§4) — they are not analyzed here. All results below are
generated by `main.R` against the real CBS extract; every number cites its source file in
`outputs/` so it can be checked directly against the repo's own artifacts.

## 1. Data Validation & Diagnostics

> **Revised 2026-09-21 (Checkpoint 13).** Every hours figure in this document was regenerated after
> `docs/decisions/hours-population-harmonization.md` harmonized the hours population across survey
> years and a follow-up redefined the Lee-bounds selection rate on the observed-hours sample. Two
> conclusions in the original version of §1 and §4 have been **withdrawn, not merely renumbered**:
> the 2017 pre-trend violation (a data defect, now corrected) and the margin-ordering / furlough
> reading of why hours move and employment does not.

**Missingness.** `WorkHoursCont` is missing for 30.7% of the analysis sample
(`outputs/comparative_stats_missing_pct.csv`). This is mechanical, not a data-quality problem: the
variable is defined only where `Employed == 1 & AvadBeshavua == 1` (`scripts/data_processing.R`),
so the rate is the non-employment rate (~22.6%) plus the ~10% of employed women who did not work in
the reference week. No separate missing-data correction is needed for `WorkHoursCont` itself; the
sample-selection concern it does create (conditioning hours on working) is handled directly by the
Lee-bounds analysis in §3, not by imputation.

**Event-study pre-trend** (`outputs/hours_diagnostics_pretrend_table.csv`,
`outputs/event_study_pretrend_hours.pdf`). Coefficients are `Mother x ShnatSeker`, reference year
2019:

| Year | Coefficient | SE | Significant? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2017 | 0.352 | 0.257 | no |
| 2018 | 0.201 | 0.219 | no |
| 2021 | 0.392 | 0.261 | no |
| 2022 | 0.121 | 0.265 | no |
| 2023 | **0.752** | 0.265 | ** |

Neither pre-period coefficient is distinguishable from zero, and the joint Wald test on the two
does **not** reject: **F(2, 63,198) = 0.949, p = 0.387**
(`outputs/pretrend_wald_hours.csv`). The same test on the *employment* outcome also passes,
F(2, 79,069) = 0.63, p = 0.53 (unchanged — employment is unaffected by the hours fix). **Parallel
trends are supported on both margins over the full 2017–2019 pre-period.**

> **WITHDRAWN: the 2017 pre-trend violation.** This section previously reported a 2017 coefficient
> of −1.524\*\*\* with a plotted CI of [−2.06, −0.98], a joint test rejecting at
> F(2, 65,088) = 23.7, p = 5.2e-11, and concluded that "the paper's parallel-trends assumption
> should be stated as resting on the 2018-2019 comparison … and the 2017 anomaly should be
> disclosed as a limitation rather than smoothed over."
>
> **That was an artifact, not a trend.** The 2017 CBS file recorded usual hours as zero for
> respondents who were employed but absent from the reference week — the code CBS otherwise uses
> for "no usual hours" — while every later year assigned them a real value. Because absence is
> mother-skewed (12.4% of employed mothers against 7.3% of childless women), roughly 5,200
> spurious zeros landed disproportionately on one side of the paper's comparison and manufactured
> exactly the pre-period divergence the test was detecting. Harmonizing the hours population on
> reference-week work removes both. The 2017 raw gap moves from −3.30 to −1.37, in line with 2018
> (−1.48) and 2019 (−1.67).
>
> Robustness: re-fitting post-harmonization on `ShnatSeker != 2017` moves the hours DiD by 0.44 SE
> and the DDD by 0.15 SE, so 2017 now behaves like any other pre-period year rather than merely
> being less contaminated. The 2018–2019 restriction has been removed from the paper throughout.

**Post-period shape.** The effect is not an immediate 2021 jump — it rises gradually, only becoming
significant by 2023 (0.752**). This pattern is itself substantively interesting and is picked up
again in §4.

**Balance tests.** The one balance check run against real data is the `GilNK` (age-group) imbalance
diagnostic (`docs/decisions/age-balance-robustness-chain.md`,
`outputs/age_balance_robustness_age_imbalance_by_quartile.csv`): the pre-period age gap between
mothers and non-mothers is **-0.974 GilNK units at the lowest WFH-exposure quartile (t = -94.0),
shrinking monotonically to +0.057 at the highest**. *Corrected 2026-09-19: this file previously
read "-0.797 ... +0.239", which predates the `BirthContinent` change to the exposure cells. The
figures above are the current CSV's and match the paper.* This gap tracks `WFH_Exposure` quartile — exactly
the hours DDD's own regressor — so an additive age control cannot fully absorb it. This is addressed
directly in §3 (age-interacted and reweighted specs, now run against real data for the hours DDD
specifically).

## 2. Main Specifications

**Primary DiD** (`outputs/intensive_margin_table.csv`, N=251,857, clustered by IDPUF):

> `Mother x Post = 0.228 (SE 0.181)` — **not significant**, t = 1.26

**There is no average intensive-margin effect.** This is a reasonably precise null rather than an
uninformative one: at this standard error the minimum detectable effect is 2.8016 × 0.181 ≈ **0.51
hours**, so average gains much above half an hour a week are ruled out. The raw 2×2 agrees (0.266,
SE 0.181), so the null is not an artifact of the control set. Both standard errors are clustered by IDPUF; the raw one is now essentially identical to the regression's.

**Primary DDD** (`outputs/ddd_hours_table.csv`, calibrated occupation-level exposure, N=246,326,
clustered by occupation):

> `Mother:Post:WFH_Exposure = 3.224** (SE 1.022)`

This is the paper's headline result, and the interpretation is **sharper than it used to be**. The
bare `Mother x Post` term inside this model is **−0.539 (ns)** — the implied hours change for
mothers in an occupation that cannot be done from home at all is, if anything, negative. Combined
with the null average DiD above, the reading is not "a broad gain that is larger in teleworkable
jobs" but **"no gain outside teleworkable jobs at all; the entire effect is the exposure
gradient."**

> **Superseded framing.** This section previously said the bare term "collapses" from 0.826 to
> 0.015 and that the average effect is "fully absorbed into exposure heterogeneity", and described
> the plain DiD as "suggestive but fragile". None of that survives: there is no average effect to
> absorb, and the DiD is a null rather than a fragile positive. The dose-response in §3 shows the
> same thing non-parametrically — three exposure quartiles at zero, one well above.

**Mechanism regression** (per-occupation `Mother:Post` point estimates regressed on occupation
`wfh_exposure`, precision-weighted by `1/se_j^2`; console output from `run_hours_ddd_regression()`,
underlying data in `outputs/hours_mechanism_data.csv`):

> slope = **2.198 (SE 0.907)**, n = 37 occupations (3 of 40 dropped for
> degenerate per-occupation fits: ISCO 62, 63, 95 — the same three before and after the fix, so the pre/post slopes are comparable). Data now exported to `outputs/hours_mechanism_data.csv`; the previously-cited `outputs/ddd_calibrated_mechanism_data.csv` is a STALE employment-outcome artifact in probability units — do not use it.

This is a second, independent piece of evidence for the same relationship, estimated at the
occupation level rather than pooled across individuals — the individual-level triple interaction and
the occupation-level mechanism slope tell the same story from two different angles.

## 3. Robustness Checks

**A note on the selection rate.** As of 2026-09-21 both Lee-bounds constructions define
`s_ab = P(hours observed | Mother, Post)` — employed **and** working the reference week — rather
than `P(Employed == 1)`. The harmonization made those two different populations, and deriving a
trim proportion on one while applying it to the other was a genuine mismatch. Fixing it also
resolved a defect: the DDD bounds had briefly *inverted* (lower above upper), which silently
tripped the degenerate branch in `imbens_manski_ci()`.

**Lee (2009) bounds, plain DiD** (`outputs/intensive_margin_lee_bounds_table.csv`): point 0.228,
bounds `[-0.517, 0.802]`, **95% Imbens-Manski CI = [-0.810, 1.096]** — crosses zero. The correction
*widens the interval around a null* rather than overturning a finding; the point estimate is
already insignificant (§2), so there is no "fragility" to flag any more.

**Lee bounds, DDD** (`outputs/hours_lee_bounds_table.csv`, `outputs/hours_lee_bounds_quartiles.csv`):
excess selection is modest in all four `WFH_Exposure` quartiles (trim proportions 0.80%-4.48%,
largest in Q1, the least-teleworkable occupations; 1,396 of 72,820 post-period mothers trimmed in
total). Bounds stay positive:

| Bound | Coef | SE | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lower | 3.126 | 1.144 | [0.883, 5.368] |
| Point | 3.232 | 1.054 | [1.166, 5.297] |
| Upper | 3.443 | 1.022 | [1.440, 5.446] |

**95% Imbens-Manski CI for the identified set = [1.021, 5.324]** (c_α = 1.840) — excludes zero
throughout. The triple interaction survives the selection correction. This is the strongest single
piece of evidence in the paper: the result is not an artifact of differential selection into the
estimation sample.

**Minimum detectable effect**: 2.864 — **88.8%** of the 3.224 point estimate, and 7.3% of the 39.18
mean weekly hours (`outputs/mde_hours.csv`, `within_mde = FALSE`). The design detects an effect of
this size, but with materially less headroom than before the harmonization (it was 76.5%). Describe
it as "inside the design's power, though not by a wide margin", not "well-powered".

**Exposure-measure sensitivity** (`outputs/ddd_hours_external.csv`, `outputs/ddd_hours_realized.csv`):

| Exposure measure | Mother:Post:WFH_Exposure | SE | Sig. |
|---|---|---|---|
| External (Dingel-Neiman) | 0.672 | 0.884 | ns |
| Calibrated (primary) | 3.224 | 1.022 | ** |
| Realized (2021 anchor) | 5.195 | 2.363 | * |

Direction is consistent across all three; magnitude and significance are not. The calibrated measure
is the one purpose-built to correct known external-index misclassification — e.g. teaching (ISCO 23)
scores 0.966 on the external index but only 0.069 realized/calibrated
(`outputs/wfh_exposure_calibrated.csv`), because Israeli schools stayed in-person by policy despite
being "teleworkable" by US task content
(`docs/decisions/calibrated-exposure-and-cell-ddd.md`). The paper should lead with the calibrated
estimate and report the other two as sensitivity, framed as "directionally robust, not point-estimate
robust."

**Age-balance robustness — now run against real data.** The hours-DDD analogs of the age-interacted
and reweighted specs (`run_hours_ddd_age_interacted()`, `run_hours_ddd_reweighted()`) had
implemented methodology but had never actually been executed against the real extract or exported
before this analysis (no corresponding CSVs existed in `outputs/`). They were run for this document
(`RUN_AGE_BALANCE_ROBUSTNESS <- TRUE`, one diagnostic rerun of `main.R`, flag reverted to its
committed `FALSE` default afterward) and exported to
`outputs/age_balance_robustness_hours_ddd_age_interacted.csv` and
`..._hours_ddd_reweighted.csv`:

| Spec | Mother:Post:WFH_Exposure | SE | Sig. |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary (no age interaction) | 3.224 | 1.022 | ** |
| Age-interacted (`+ Mother:GilNK`) | 3.205 | 1.055 | ** |
| Reweighted (pre-period `GilNK`-raking) | 2.597 | 1.050 | * |

The triple interaction **survives both checks**: barely moved by adding `Mother:GilNK` (3.224 →
3.205), and attenuated but still significant under reweighting (3.224 → 2.597, a **19.4%**
reduction). ⚠ **Note the reweighted spec is now significant at 5%, not 1%** — any text saying
"still significant at p<0.01" is false. At 2.597 it also sits only narrowly above the MDE of 2.864,
so the right characterisation is "robust to age imbalance in direction and approximate magnitude,
not insensitive to it". The confirmed age imbalance (§1) does not overturn the headline result,
though the reweighted attenuation is worth reporting rather than omitting — it's the largest single
move any robustness check produces on this coefficient.

A secondary finding falls out of the age-interacted spec: `Mother x GilNK4 = -2.266*** (0.506)`,
`Mother x GilNK5 = -1.150* (0.476)`, `Mother x GilNK6 = 0.419 (ns)`, `Mother x GilNK7 = 0.963
(0.557, 10% only)` — a rising age pattern in the hours penalty itself, independent of WFH exposure,
significant only in groups 4 and 5. (The earlier "significant, non-monotonic" reading no longer
fits the current coefficients.) This mirrors a
similar age-heterogeneity finding already documented for the employment DDD
(`docs/decisions/age-balance-robustness-chain.md`) and is worth a sentence in the paper as a
distinct, incidental finding, not a rescue or complication of the main WFH-mechanism result.

**ISCO disclosure-masking sensitivity** (`outputs/isco_masking_sensitivity_by_group.csv`,
`_comparison_wide.csv`): the masked-vs-unmasked realized-WFH gap is small and inconsistently signed
across ISCO1 major groups (+0.134 in group 2 (professionals), -0.086 in group 6, -0.085 in group 5,
etc.), and the pooled regression coefficient on `ISCO_masked` is only marginally significant (0.045*,
SE 0.021) with a negligible within-R² (9.45e-5). This is reassuring but weak evidence — report as "no
strong evidence that disclosure masking biases the exposure measure," not as a clean bill of health.

**Known, un-fixable limitations to state plainly in the paper:**
- Lee bounds here only correct *excess* selection in the `Mother=1,Post=1` cell; under-selection is a
  missing-data problem this construction cannot address
  (`docs/decisions/intensive-margin-lee-bounds.md`).
- The Lee-bounds counterfactual relies on the same parallel-trends assumption as the outcome
  equation. As of 2026-09-21 that assumption is supported over the full pre-period (§1); it is no longer "dented by the 2017 finding".
- Monotone selection is assumed (WFH weakly increases, never decreases, employment probability).
- No CBS survey weights (`MishkalSofi`, etc.) are applied anywhere in this project's outcome
  regressions — a deliberate, project-wide scope decision (`CLAUDE.md`, README "Known limitations"),
  not something specific to this result.

## 4. Economic Interpretation

Among mothers who remain in work, there is **no post-2021 hours effect on average**, and a large one
**in teleworkable occupations**. The conjunction is the result: a triple-interaction coefficient of
~3.2 additional weekly hours per unit of exposure, an implied change at zero exposure that is if
anything negative, and a Lee-bounds correction the estimate survives. This is consistent with WFH
access relaxing a binding time constraint specifically for mothers in teleworkable jobs, and is hard
to reconcile with a general post-pandemic recovery common to all women — which would have raised
hours across occupations rather than only where the work can be done from home.

Note the framing is stronger than "not uniform across occupations", which was the pre-2026-09-21
wording. "Not uniform" describes an aggregate gain distributed unevenly; there is no aggregate gain.
The effect is *absent* outside teleworkable jobs and *present* only inside them.

**Which occupations are doing the work.** The calibration step (`calibrate_isco_exposure()`) swapped
10 of 40 ISCO-2 occupations away from their external Dingel-Neiman score toward realized Israeli
behavior where the gap was large and statistically distinguishable from noise
(`outputs/wfh_exposure_calibrated.csv`). Concretely: clerical support (ISCO 41, calibrated 0.098,
down from external 1.0) and teaching (ISCO 23, calibrated 0.069, down from 0.966) were corrected
sharply downward — both occupations the external index over-predicts as teleworkable relative to
Israeli institutional practice — while occupations like ICT professionals (ISCO 25, calibrated 0.360,
external 1.0) remained high on both measures. This grounds `WFH_Exposure` in concrete, checkable
occupational content rather than leaving it as an abstract regressor.

**Timing.** The gradual rise from an insignificant 2021 estimate to a significant 2023 one (§1) is
consistent with WFH adoption and household time-reallocation being a multi-year adjustment process,
not an instantaneous response to the initial 2020-21 shock — plausible given that occupational WFH
norms themselves were still settling over 2021-2023 (`docs/decisions/checkpoint6-wfh-anchor-year.md`
makes the same point about the exposure measure's own anchor-year choice).

**Why hours, not employment.** This project's primary specification pivoted from the extensive
margin (employment) to the intensive margin (hours) specifically because the employment DDD remained
underpowered even after two rounds of exposure-measure fixes (MDE ~26% of baseline, roughly 4x the
point estimate — `docs/decisions/hours-ddd-pivot.md`). The hours DDD is detectable, though with less
headroom than before (MDE 88.8% of the point estimate, §3). The economic story remains coherent with
the pivot, but it must now be stated with the teleworkability qualifier: the effect operates on
*how much* mothers **in teleworkable jobs** work, conditional on working, not on *whether* they are
employed at all.

> **WITHDRAWN: the margin-ordering / furlough reading.** Earlier versions of this section, and of
> the paper's Discussion, argued that labor supply adjusts on the cheaper margin — hours — before
> the costlier one of leaving employment, with Israel's furlough (*Halat*) system offered as
> institutional support. **That account no longer fits the data.** Margin-ordering predicts an
> *average* hours response, and there is none; it also says nothing about why the response we do
> find is graded by teleworkability. The Goldin (2014) time-constraint mechanism explains the
> exposure gradient directly and now carries the interpretation alone.
>
> What survives, and arguably gains weight, is the purely statistical account: the continuous hours
> outcome carries strictly more information per observation than a binary employment indicator, so
> the contrast between the two margins may be as much about what each design can see as about what
> workers did.

## 5. Subgroup Heterogeneity and Gender Placebo

Arab vs. Jewish women (Leom == 2 / Leom == 1) and a male gender placebo ("Mother" read as "Father").
Both use the same primary specifications as §2 — `run_intensive_margin_reg()` for the DiD,
`run_hours_ddd_regression()` (or its placebo analog) for the DDD — applied to different subsamples,
with no new econometric machinery. Point estimates below are exact values from
`outputs/hours_did_subgroup_comparison_data.csv` and `outputs/hours_ddd_subgroup_comparison_data.csv`
(4 significant figures; console/CSV table output rounds further). All cross-group tests below are

sampling covariance is exactly zero, not merely assumed small.

### 5.2 Is the Arab DDD estimate (5.507) genuinely larger than the Jewish estimate (2.847), or a small-sample artifact?

The raw point estimates suggest the mechanism is roughly twice as strong for Arab women. Two
of the three pieces of evidence below argue this reading is not supportable, and that the gap is best explained by
Arab women's much smaller, more occupationally-concentrated subsample rather than a genuinely
larger effect.

**(a) The two coefficients are not statistically distinguishable.** A two-sample z-test on the DDD
term:

> z = (5.5069 − 2.8465) / sqrt(2.6741² + 0.9125²) = 2.6604 / 2.8255 = **0.942, p = 0.346**

fails to reject equality at any conventional level. The apparent gap is well within what Arab
women's own sampling noise can produce — Arab's SE (2.643) is nearly 3x Jewish's (0.890), driven
by the ~8.6x smaller sample (24,307 vs. 209,727 occupation-matched rows with observed hours). The same test on
the plain DiD term (0.9029 vs. 0.1267) gives z = 1.488, p = 0.137 — also not significant, though
here the *direction* matters too: Arab women's own plain-DiD estimate is statistically
indistinguishable from zero (0.1267, SE 0.4798), so there is no significant average hours effect
for Arab women to begin with; the DDD's marginal significance (see (b)) rests entirely on the
cross-occupation exposure gradient, not a broad group-level shift.

**(b) The Arab DDD's own occupation-level mechanism check contradicts its sign.**
`run_hours_ddd_regression()`'s second-stage mechanism regression (per-occupation `Mother:Post`
estimates regressed on occupation `wfh_exposure`, precision-weighted) is the direct test of whether
the triple interaction's implied cross-occupation gradient actually holds within the subgroup. **Recomputed 2026-09-21 against the harmonized data.** All three slopes below are current:

| Subgroup | Slope | SE | t | p | R² | n | Dropped |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full sample | +2.198 | 0.907 | 2.42 | 0.021 | 0.144 | 37 | 3 (ISCO 62, 63, 95) |
| Jewish women | +3.571 | 2.365 | 1.51 | 0.140 | 0.061 | 37 | 3 (ISCO 62, 63, 95) |
| Arab women | **+7.572** | 2.383 | 3.18 | 0.004 | 0.305 | 25 | **15 (37.5%)** |

> **⚠ THIS ARGUMENT HAS REVERSED. The Arab mechanism slope changed sign.** Before the
> harmonization it was **−19.29 (SE 7.04), p = 0.011, n = 26**, and this section argued that "an
> estimate whose own internal mechanism check contradicts its headline sign is not merely
> imprecise; it is actively uncorroborated by within-subgroup evidence." **That is no longer
> true.** At +7.572 the Arab mechanism slope is positive, significant, and consistent in sign with
> the Arab triple interaction (+5.507) — the within-subgroup check now *corroborates* the Arab DDD
> rather than contradicting it. Argument (b) must be withdrawn as an objection.
>
> The reversal also cuts the other way for the Jewish estimate, whose slope went from a marginal
> +2.148 (p = 0.067) to an insignificant **+3.571 (p = 0.140)** with roughly double the standard
> error. So the neat pre-fix picture — Jewish corroborated, Arab contradicted — has become: both
> positive, Arab better identified within-subgroup than Jewish.

What survives is the **occupational-coverage** objection, and it is if anything slightly stronger:
an Arab-specific `Mother:Post` estimate can be fitted in only **25 of 40** occupations (37.5%
attrition, against 7.5% for the full sample and for Jewish women). With 25 informative occupations
and a much smaller subsample, a few high-leverage occupations can move the slope a long way — which
is exactly what the pre/post-harmonization swing from −19.29 to +7.572 demonstrates about its
stability. The Arab result remains imprecisely identified; it is no longer *contradicted* by its
own mechanism check, but neither slope is stable enough to carry a heterogeneity claim.

**(c) A plausible mechanical cause: occupational concentration.** The 14-of-40 (35%) occupation
dropout rate for Arab women, versus 3-of-40 (7.5%) for Jewish/the full sample, is consistent with
the well-documented concentration of Arab women's employment in Israel into a narrower set of
occupations (disproportionately education and health-aide roles) — leaving far fewer than 40
occupations with enough Arab respondents to fit a stable per-occupation `Mother:Post` estimate at
all. This is the same small-cell-instability failure mode this project's exposure-calibration step
was built to guard against at the *occupation* level (`docs/decisions/calibrated-exposure-and-cell-ddd.md`'s
Problem 2 — a 4-observation ISCO-63 cell swinging an entire ranking); here it resurfaces at the
*subgroup* level instead. With only 26 occupations carrying real information, a small number of
high-leverage occupations can dominate — and, per (b), evidently do flip — the sign of the
mechanism regression, and by extension undermine confidence in the DDD coefficient it is supposed
to corroborate.

**Conclusion for the paper.** Report the Arab DDD point estimate (5.507*, nominally significant on
its own one-sample test) transparently, but do **not** characterize it as
evidence that the WFH-exposure mechanism is stronger for Arab women than for Jewish women. The
correct statement, **revised 2026-09-21**, is: the Arab estimate is imprecisely estimated,
statistically indistinguishable from the Jewish estimate, and rests on only 25 of 40 occupations —
a small-sample/thin-occupational-coverage fragility, not a documented heterogeneous treatment
effect.

Note what can **no longer** be said. The pre-harmonization version of this conclusion added "and
contradicted by its own occupation-level mechanism check", which was the strongest of the three
objections. It is now false: the Arab mechanism slope is +7.572 (p = 0.004), positive and
consistent with the Arab DDD's own sign. Nor can the Jewish estimate still be described as
"corroborated directionally by its own mechanism regression" — its slope is now insignificant
(+3.571, p = 0.140). Both subgroup mechanism slopes are positive; neither is stable. The
conclusion survives on arguments (a) and (c), but it is a weaker case than it was, and the
paper's §6.4 — which rests on exactly those two and never cited the mechanism check — is
unaffected.

### 5.3 Gender placebo: does the mechanism validate as motherhood-specific?

`hours_gender_placebo.R`'s design (see its header comment) exists to distinguish three competing
explanations for the primary result: (i) a genuine motherhood-specific mechanism, (ii) a general
parenthood effect that would appear symmetrically for fathers, or (iii) a macro, sex-and-parenthood-
agnostic post-2021 hours trend that happens to correlate with occupational WFH exposure for
unrelated reasons. Re-running the primary DiD/DDD on the male subsample, reading `Mother` as
"Father," discriminates between these: (ii) and (iii) both predict a placebo coefficient similar in
sign and magnitude to the women's estimate; (i) predicts it should not.

The data support (i). Formal two-sample z-tests (independent samples: women vs. men are disjoint by
construction):

| Comparison | DDD term | DiD term |
|---|---|---|
| All women (primary) vs. Men (placebo) | z = 3.461, **p = 0.0005** | z = 2.406, **p = 0.016** |
| Jewish women vs. Men (placebo) | z = 3.376, **p = 0.0007** | z = 2.538, **p = 0.011** |
| Arab women vs. Men (placebo) | z = 2.541, **p = 0.011** | z = 0.637, p = 0.524 (ns) |

All six rows recomputed 2026-09-21 from the current subgroup CSVs. **Note the Arab-vs-men DiD row
is now insignificant** (z = 0.637) — so the blanket claim that every female subgroup is
distinguishable from the placebo on both margins no longer holds, and is withdrawn below.

**The placebo now behaves differently on the two margins, and the two must be reported separately.**

- **On the DDD it does its job, and more than the minimum.** `Mother:Post:WFH_Exposure` (i.e.
  Father) = **-1.762 (SE 1.015)**, N = 251,779 — negative, significant only at the 10% level, and
  significantly different from the women's +3.224 (z = 3.461, p = 0.0005). Fathers show no positive
  WFH-linked hours response. A macro trend common to all workers cannot produce a positive exposure
  gradient for mothers alongside a negative one for fathers, so (iii) is ruled out on this margin.
  **This is where the mechanism claim rests.**
- **On the plain DiD the placebo is ADVERSE, and the paper says so rather than letting a reader
  discover it.** `Mother:Post` (Father:Post) = **-0.400\* (SE 0.188)**, N = 261,543 — *larger in
  magnitude and more statistically significant than the women's insignificant +0.228*. The z-test
  still rejects (z = 2.406, p = 0.016), but for the wrong reason: the difference is manufactured by
  the fathers' coefficient, not the mothers'. A parenthood-common trend — parents of both sexes
  losing hours relative to non-parents, mothers by less — is a live alternative here. A design
  whose placebo moves more than its treatment group has not demonstrated a clean average effect on
  that margin, and the paper does not claim one; it reads the female DiD as a null (§2), and the
  placebo is consistent with that reading rather than against it.

> **WITHDRAWN: "every female subgroup is distinguishable from the placebo on both margins, which is
> stronger than the placebo being insignificant."** That held when the female DiD was +0.826\*\*\*
> against the male −0.386\*: two meaningful coefficients of opposite sign. With the female DiD now a
> null, the sentence is technically true but misleading, and has been rewritten in the paper to
> scope the claim to the triple interaction.

**Limitations of this placebo, stated plainly.** (1) No second-stage occupation-level mechanism
regression was run for the male placebo — `run_hours_gender_ddd_placebo()` does not implement one
(unlike `run_hours_ddd_regression()`), so §5.2's corroboration-check logic has no male analog in
this pipeline; the placebo rests on the triple-interaction point estimate alone. (2) Men's labor
supply is shaped by institutional factors this project's controls do not model (e.g., differential
reserve-duty exposure, different typical retirement timing) that could independently move men's
hours across 2017-2023 for reasons unrelated to WFH or fatherhood; `DEFAULT_CONTROLS` does not
purge this, though nothing about it points toward the specific pattern observed (a WFH-exposure
gradient with the opposite sign from mothers'). (3) The z-tests above compare independently-fit
coefficients rather than jointly estimating a single fully-interacted `Mother*Post*WFH_Exposure*Sex`
model; the independent-samples covariance argument makes this valid, but a joint specification
sharing `DEFAULT_CONTROLS` across sexes would be a natural next robustness step if reviewers ask for
one model rather than two compared post hoc.

**Conclusion for the paper.** The gender-placebo evidence is a genuine, positive result, not just an
absence of a counter-finding: fathers show no positive WFH-linked hours response, and if anything a
small negative post-2021 hours shift. Combined with §5.2's ethnicity finding, the paper's
defensible subgroup claim is: *the hours-WFH mechanism is well-supported for women generally and for
Jewish women specifically, is not corroborated by within-subgroup mechanism evidence for Arab women
despite a nominally significant point estimate, and is absent (or reversed) for men* — supporting a
motherhood-specific, not a general parenthood- or macro-driven, interpretation.
